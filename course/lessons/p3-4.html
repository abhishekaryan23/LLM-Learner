Embeddings

Lesson 4 of Neural nets → language

A token ID like 73700 is just a name tag. Neural networks need numbers they can do math with, so the first layer of every LLM swaps each ID for a learned vector. Training arranges those vectors so that similar tokens point in similar directions.

By the end you can

  • Explain why an embedding layer is "one-hot times a matrix", and why in practice it's just a row lookup.
  • Compute cosine similarity and say what it does and doesn't tell you about two tokens.
  • Explain how embeddings are learned, and how the output layer (unembedding) and weight tying relate to them.
Builds on: Tokenization (where the IDs come from), Vectors & dot products (cosine similarity), Matrices as transformations and Softmax (the output end of the model).
Warm-up · guess before you learn

Guess, then find out.

Where do the numbers in a model's embedding table come from?

  1. A dictionary of hand-written word features
  2. The characters' Unicode codes
  3. They start random and are learned by gradient descent, like every other weight
  4. They are recomputed from each sentence
They're ordinary parameters. Training on the next-token objective pushes them into a useful arrangement; nobody designs them.

GPT-2 small has 50,257 tokens and 768-dimensional vectors. About how many parameters is its embedding table?

  1. About 39 million
  2. About 50 thousand
  3. About 768 thousand
  4. About 4 billion
One row of 768 numbers per token: $50{,}257 \times 768 \approx 38.6$ million, roughly 31% of GPT-2 small's 124 million parameters.

From IDs to vectors: a lookup table

The embedding table is a matrix $E$ with one row per vocabulary entry: $V$ rows and $d$ columns, where $V$ is the vocabulary size and $d$ the model width. Token $i$'s vector is simply row $i$.

Mathematically, that's a matrix multiply. Write the token as a one-hot row vector $x$ (length $V$, a 1 at position $i$, zeros elsewhere). Then

$$e = xE = E_{i,:}$$

Every row of $E$ is multiplied by 0 except row $i$, so the product is row $i$. Real code skips the multiply, which would waste $V \times d$ multiplications on zeros, and just indexes the row (PyTorch's nn.Embedding). Thinking of it as $xE$ is still useful: it shows the embedding is a linear layer (P1.2) and tells you how its gradient works.

Real sizes: GPT-2 small's table is $50{,}257 \times 768 \approx 38.6$M parameters. Llama 3 8B's is $128{,}256 \times 4{,}096 \approx 525$M. The input embedding has no idea about word order; position information is added separately (M1.3).

Explain like I'm 5

Every word gets a home address on a big town map. Animals live in the park, fruit lives at the market, vehicles live at the station.

Nobody planned the town. At first everyone was dropped in a random spot. Then, again and again, words that turned up in the same kinds of sentences were nudged closer together. After a long time, similar things ended up as neighbours.

So to guess what a word is like, look at who lives next door.

Where it breaks: the real "town" has hundreds or thousands of directions instead of two, and one word can be near different neighbours along different directions.

Similarity is an angle

To compare two embeddings, we usually ignore their lengths and look at the angle between them, the cosine similarity from P1.1:

$$\cos(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert b\rVert}$$

$a \cdot b$ is the dot product and $\lVert a\rVert$ the length of $a$. The result runs from $1$ (same direction) through $0$ (perpendicular, unrelated) to $-1$ (opposite). Lengths are divided out because they often track things other than meaning, such as how frequent a token is.

Worked example

$a = (1,\,2,\,2)$ and $b = (2,\,1,\,2)$.

  1. Dot product: $1\cdot2 + 2\cdot1 + 2\cdot2 = 8$
  2. Lengths: $\lVert a\rVert = \sqrt{1+4+4} = 3$, and $\lVert b\rVert = 3$ too
  3. $\cos(a,b) = 8 / (3 \times 3) \approx 0.89$: quite similar ✓

Embeddings are learned

The table starts as small random numbers. Training on next-token prediction (M2.2) then adjusts it by backpropagation (P2.3), like any other weight. Because $e = xE$ with a one-hot $x$, the gradient for the input table touches only the rows of tokens that appeared in the batch; all other rows get zero gradient for that step.

Why do similar words end up close? Tokens that appear in similar contexts must lead to similar predictions, and the cheapest way for the network to do that is to give them similar vectors. That is the old distributional hypothesis from linguistics: "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth, 1957).

One important limit: the input embedding is static. "bank" gets the same row whether the sentence is about rivers or money. Making it context-dependent is the job of the attention layers that come next (M1.1).

The famous analogy, with caveats

With word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013), vector arithmetic seemed to capture relations: the nearest word to $\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman}$ was "queen", and similarly for capitals and countries or verb tenses. It's a striking result, but read it carefully:

  • The inputs are excluded. The standard test ignores king, man and woman when searching for the nearest word. Without that rule, the nearest vector is often "king" itself (Nissim, van Noord & van der Goot, 2020).
  • It works for some relations much better than others. Tidy results like gender or capital cities don't hold up evenly across relation types (Linzen, 2016).
  • Embeddings absorb biases in their training text: the same arithmetic produced "man : computer programmer :: woman : homemaker" (Bolukbasi et al., 2016).
  • LLM token embeddings are a different setting. Their tokens are word pieces, and most of the meaning is built up by later layers, so don't expect clean analogies in a raw LLM embedding table.

The other end: unembedding and weight tying

At the top of the model, the final hidden vector $h$ (length $d$) has to become one logit per vocabulary entry for the softmax (P3.2). That's another matrix, the unembedding $W_U$ of shape $d \times V$:

$$\text{logits} = h\,W_U, \qquad \text{logit}_i = h \cdot u_i$$

where $u_i$ is column $i$ of $W_U$, one vector per token. So the output layer also works by similarity: the next token is likely when the model's final vector points the same way as that token's output vector (and, because it is a dot product rather than a cosine, longer vectors count for more).

Since $E$ and $W_U$ both hold one $d$-sized vector per token, you can make them the same matrix, $W_U = E^\top$. This weight tying (Press & Wolf, 2017; Inan et al., 2017) was used in the original transformer and in GPT-2, and saves $V \times d$ parameters: 38.6M for GPT-2 small. Small models such as Gemma tie their embeddings; Llama 2 and Llama 3 8B keep the two separate.

Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Explain what an embedding layer does, and why similar tokens end up with similar vectors.

Model answer: It looks up row $i$ of a learned $V \times d$ table for token $i$, which is the same as multiplying a one-hot vector by the table. The table starts random and is trained with the rest of the model; tokens that appear in similar contexts need to lead to similar predictions, so gradient descent pushes their vectors in similar directions.
Check yourself

For a vocabulary of size $V$ and model width $d$, multiplying a one-hot vector by the embedding table $E$ gives…

  1. a $V \times d$ matrix
  2. one row of $E$, a vector of length $d$
  3. the sum of all rows of $E$
  4. a probability distribution over the vocabulary
$(1 \times V)(V \times d) = 1 \times d$. All rows are multiplied by 0 except the one with the 1, so the result is that row.

Two embeddings have cosine similarity close to 0. That means they are…

  1. identical
  2. exact opposites
  3. both very short vectors
  4. roughly perpendicular: they point in unrelated directions
Cosine is the dot product divided by both lengths (P1.1). 1 means same direction, −1 opposite, and 0 perpendicular. Length is divided out, so short vectors don't make it 0.

During one training step, the batch contains only 3,000 distinct tokens out of a 50,000-token vocabulary. Which rows of the input embedding table get a nonzero gradient?

  1. Only the 3,000 rows for tokens in the batch
  2. All 50,000 rows equally
  3. None. Embeddings are frozen after initialisation.
  4. Only the row of the most frequent token
With $e = xE$ and a one-hot $x$, a row only affects the loss if its token appeared. (With weight tying the same matrix also serves as the output layer, whose softmax does send a gradient to every row.)

The final hidden vector is $h$, and the unembedding has a column $u_i$ for each token. The next-token probabilities are…

  1. the entries of $h$ directly
  2. $\cos(h, u_i)$ for each $i$, which already sum to 1
  3. $\mathrm{softmax}$ of the logits $h \cdot u_i$ (P3.2)
  4. the row of $E$ closest to $h$, with probability 1
The logits are dot products $h\cdot u_i$, and softmax turns them into a distribution. Cosines don't sum to 1, and the model gives every token some probability.

Key takeaways

  • The embedding table $E$ is $V \times d$; token $i$'s vector is row $i$, the same as one-hot $\times E$.
  • Embeddings are learned by the training objective; tokens in similar contexts end up pointing in similar directions.
  • Cosine similarity compares directions: 1 same, 0 unrelated, −1 opposite.
  • Analogy arithmetic (king − man + woman ≈ queen) is real but fragile: it excludes the inputs, varies by relation and reflects bias.
  • The unembedding $W_U$ maps the final vector to logits by dot products; weight tying sets $W_U = E^\top$.
