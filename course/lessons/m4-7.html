Building your own evals

Lesson 7 of Evaluation

A public benchmark tells you how a model does on someone else's problem. It can't tell you whether your support bot quotes your refund policy correctly, calls the right tool, or keeps its JSON valid. For that you need your own eval suite, built from your product's real failures. In practice, that suite becomes the spec for what "good" means.

By the end you can

  • Turn real usage and real failures into test cases, golden answers and rubrics.
  • Choose between code assertions, model-graded rubrics and human review for each check.
  • Run evals as a development loop and as a regression gate in CI, with versioned eval sets and without overfitting to them.
Builds on: Benchmarks (what an eval is), LLM-as-judge (model-graded checks and their biases) and Statistical rigor (how much a small eval set can tell you).
Warm-up · guess before you learn

Take your best guess. Being wrong now helps the right answer stick later.

You're about to improve a customer-support bot. Where should your first eval cases come from?

  1. The most popular public benchmark
  2. 1,000 random questions generated by an LLM
  3. Real conversations, especially the ones that went wrong
  4. Edge cases the team brainstorms in a meeting
Real traffic shows the failures that actually happen, at the rates they happen. Brainstormed and synthetic cases are useful additions, but they miss surprising real failures and over-represent imagined ones.

Which check is best done with plain code rather than an LLM judge?

  1. "The reply is valid JSON with an order_id field"
  2. "The reply sounds empathetic"
  3. "The explanation is clear to a beginner"
  4. "The summary captures the key points"
If a property can be checked exactly, check it with code: it's free, instant, deterministic and unbiased. Save judges for genuine judgement calls.

Why generic benchmarks aren't enough

An MMLU or SWE-bench score measures broad capability (M4.2). Your product succeeds or fails on narrower things: your documents, your tools, your users' phrasing, your output format, your policies. Two models with the same leaderboard rank can differ wildly on those. Public benchmarks also saturate and leak into training data (M4.3), while your own cases are private and specific.

A useful way to think about it: the eval suite is the specification. Writing a test case forces you to decide what the right behaviour is. Most "the model is bad" complaints turn out to be "we never wrote down what good looks like".

Start from real failure modes

Don't start with generic metrics like "helpfulness" or "toxicity". Start by reading what your system actually does:

  1. Collect a sample of real traces: production logs (with consent, and with private data scrubbed), support tickets, thumbs-down feedback, internal dogfooding.
  2. Read them, 50 to 100 to start, and write a one-line note on every failure. This is tedious, and it's where most of the value is.
  3. Group the notes into categories ("wrong refund window", "invalid JSON", "called lookup with the wrong ID") and count them.
  4. Prioritise by frequency × severity. A rare failure that leaks private data outranks a common one that's merely awkward.

Expect your criteria to change as you read. A study of people grading LLM outputs found that they refine what they consider a failure while grading ("criteria drift"; Shankar et al., 2024, "Who Validates the Validators?"). That's a reason to look at data before you finalise rubrics, not after.

Explain like I'm 5

A baker once won a big trophy for "best cake in the country". Nice! But their customers come for croissants: flaky, not too sweet, warm by 7 o'clock.

So the baker made a checklist with pictures of what a perfect croissant looks like, and tastes every batch against it. When a customer says "this one was soggy", the baker adds a soggy-croissant picture to the list, so it never sneaks through again.

The trophy sits on the shelf. The checklist is what keeps the bakery good.

Where it breaks: a baker can taste every pastry. An LLM answers millions of different questions, so your checklist only ever covers a sample, and you need enough cases (and the statistics from M4.6) to trust it.

Write test cases

A test case is an input (the conversation, any retrieved documents or tool state) plus a definition of pass. It also carries metadata: category, severity, source (production or synthetic), and the date it was added. "Pass" comes in three flavours:

  • Golden answer: a human-verified reference output. It's ideal when there is one right answer (extraction, classification, a SQL query's result). For open-ended replies, use the reference as guidance for a grader, not for exact match.
  • Properties: things that must or must not be true. "Calls lookup_order with ID 4417", "under 120 words", "never mentions a competitor".
  • Rubric: a short list of specific criteria for judgement calls, graded by a model or a person.

Cover several kinds of input. Include typical requests (most of your traffic), every known failure category, edge cases (very long inputs, other languages, typos, prompt injection in a pasted document), and must-not cases (requests the bot should decline or hand to a human). You can use an LLM to generate variations of real cases along chosen dimensions (persona × intent × difficulty), but review what it writes. Don't let synthetic cases replace real ones.

Grading: assertions, rubrics, humans

Use the cheapest grader that's reliable for each check:

  1. Code assertions: exact match, regex, JSON-schema validation, unit tests run against generated code, the tool name and arguments, numeric tolerance, length and latency limits, forbidden strings (e.g. card numbers). They're deterministic, free and unbiased. Use them for everything they can check.
  2. Model-graded rubrics (M4.4): for qualities code can't check. Make each criterion specific and close to yes/no. Write "states the 30-day return window" and "doesn't promise a refund before checking the order", not "rate helpfulness 1–10". Give the judge the reference answer, ask for its reasoning before the verdict, and validate the judge against a hundred or so human labels before trusting it. Re-validate whenever the rubric or the judge model changes.
  3. Human review: the gold standard (M4.5), but slow. Use it to label the calibration set for judges, for high-stakes categories, and for regular spot checks of what the automated graders pass.

Worked example: grading one reply with a rubric

Prompt: "I bought shoes 3 weeks ago and they're falling apart, can I return them?" The rubric has four yes/no criteria, and the case passes if all four hold:

  1. States the 30-day return window: yes.
  2. Asks for the order number before promising anything: yes.
  3. Doesn't invent a policy that isn't in the docs (the bot offered "free express shipping on the replacement", which isn't a policy): no.
  4. Professional, friendly tone: yes.
  5. Score 3/4, so the case fails. The failing criterion tells you exactly what to fix. A single 1–10 "quality" score would have said "8/10" and hidden it.

The eval-driven development loop

This is test-driven development with scores instead of a single pass/fail:

  1. Write the cases first for the behaviour you want, and run them on the current system to get a baseline.
  2. Change one thing: the prompt, the model, retrieval, a tool, a fine-tune.
  3. Re-run everything, not just the category you targeted, and compare per category on the same cases (a paired comparison, M4.6).
  4. Read the diffs: every case that flipped from pass to fail, or fail to pass. The average hides what changed; the flips show it.
  5. Ship if the target improved and nothing important regressed. Then monitor production, mine new failures, and add them as cases.

When a new model is released, this suite tells you within an hour whether it's better for you. No leaderboard can do that.

Version your eval sets

  • Keep the eval set in version control like code: a stable ID per case, a changelog, and a version number on every result ("suite v1.3, judge prompt v2, judge model X, 3 samples per case").
  • Scores on different versions aren't comparable. When you add or fix cases, re-run the baseline on the new version before comparing.
  • Expect some labels to be wrong. Golden answers have mistakes, so give people a way to dispute a case, and fix it in a new version.
  • Freeze a version for reporting, and keep a growing working version for development.

Don't overfit to your own evals

Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Forty rounds of prompt tweaks against the same 60 cases will teach the prompt those cases' quirks, and the score stops predicting what users experience.

  • Split into a dev set you iterate on and a held-out set you check rarely, ideally only before a release.
  • Refresh regularly with new production cases. Old cases go stale as your traffic changes.
  • Test behaviour, not wording. An assertion on an exact sentence breaks for harmless rephrasings and rewards copying.
  • Never paste eval cases into the prompt as few-shot examples. That's contamination (M4.3) of your own benchmark.
  • Watch production signals (thumbs-down rate, escalations to humans) next to eval scores. If they diverge, your evals have drifted from reality.

Track regressions in CI

  • Tiers. Run a fast smoke suite (assertions plus a small judged set) on every pull request. Run the full suite nightly and before every release, within a cost budget.
  • Gate rules. Hard-fail on any critical case: a privacy leak, invalid JSON on a known-good case, an unsafe answer. For the rest, use per-category thresholds compared against the baseline on the same cases. Flag only drops larger than the noise (M4.6), so the gate doesn't cry wolf.
  • Non-determinism. Even at temperature 0, many model APIs aren't perfectly deterministic. Re-run flaky cases several times, and pin model versions: a silently updated model is a regression you didn't make.
  • History. Store results per commit, so you can chart each category over time and see exactly which change broke what.

Open-source harnesses such as OpenAI Evals, Inspect (from the UK AI Security Institute) and promptfoo implement this plumbing. The harness is the easy part. Good cases and validated graders are the hard part.

Why this matters in practice: teams that ship reliable LLM features tend to spend most of their eval effort on data (reading traces, writing and fixing cases, labelling judge calibration sets), not on harness code or dashboards. The same suite drives prompt changes, model upgrades and fine-tunes. Safety and agent behaviour get the same treatment with extra care, which is the next lesson (M4.8).
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Why should eval cases come from your product's real failures rather than generic quality metrics? And how do you stop the suite from being overfit?

Model answer: Real failures show what actually goes wrong for your users and how often, so fixing them moves what matters. Generic metrics can stay high while specific, costly failures slip through. To avoid overfitting, iterate on a dev set and check a held-out set rarely, refresh cases from new production data, test behaviour rather than exact wording, never put eval cases into prompts, and watch whether eval scores still track production signals.
Check yourself

Which grader fits "the reply must never contain a full 16-digit card number"?

  1. An LLM judge asked "is this reply safe to send?"
  2. A regex assertion on every reply, as a hard-fail check
  3. A monthly human review of 20 random replies
  4. A 1–10 safety score from a rubric judge
It's exactly checkable, critical and cheap to test, so it should be a deterministic assertion that blocks merges. A judge might miss it, and a monthly sample would catch it far too late.

Prompt v2 raises the overall pass rate from 81% to 83% on 200 cases, but the 20-case "refunds" category falls from 90% to 70%. What should you do?

  1. Ship: the overall pass rate went up by 2 points
  2. Ignore it: 20 cases is too few to mean anything
  3. Delete the refund cases that now fail, as outdated
  4. Read the refund cases that flipped to fail before deciding
Twenty cases are noisy (each pass rate has roughly ±13–20 points of 95% CI), but four refund cases flipping to fail is a signal worth reading. A category regression can hide inside an overall gain, and refunds may matter more than the categories that improved. Deleting failing cases is overfitting by another name.

After 40 rounds of prompt tweaks on the same 60 cases, the score is 97%, yet user complaints are rising. What's the most likely explanation?

  1. The prompt overfit those 60 cases, so they no longer reflect real traffic
  2. The users are wrong: a 97% score proves quality
  3. The judge model is too strict on the hard cases
  4. 60 cases is too many to iterate on effectively
This is Goodhart's law. Iterating against a fixed small set tunes the system to its quirks. A held-out set, regular refreshes from production, and watching production signals are the defences.

Your rubric judge compares the v1 and v2 replies side by side, and always shows v1 first. Recalling LLM-as-judge (M4.4), what's the risk?

  1. Length bias in favour of v1
  2. Self-preference: the judge favours its own model family's style
  3. Position bias: the judge may favour one slot, so swap the order and average
  4. None, as long as the judge is a strong, recent model
LLM judges are known to be sensitive to answer order. Showing v1 first every time builds that bias into every comparison. Swap positions (judge both orders) or randomise, and count inconsistent verdicts as ties.

Key takeaways

  • Your eval suite is your product's spec. Build it from real traces and failures: read, categorise, count, prioritise.
  • A case = input + definition of pass (golden answer, properties or rubric) + metadata.
  • Use the cheapest reliable grader: code assertions first, specific validated rubrics for judgement calls, humans to calibrate.
  • Loop: baseline → change one thing → re-run all → read the flips → ship → mine new failures. Gate merges in CI with noise-aware thresholds.
  • Version eval sets, keep a held-out split, and refresh from production to avoid overfitting.
