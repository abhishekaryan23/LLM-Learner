LoRA & QLoRA

Lesson 7 of Post-training

Fine-tuning every weight of a 7-billion-parameter model needs over 100 GB of GPU memory. LoRA freezes the model and trains a tiny low-rank "patch" instead, often well under 1% of the parameters. QLoRA goes further and stores the frozen model in 4 bits. Together they made fine-tuning possible on a single GPU.

By the end you can

  • Explain where full fine-tuning's memory goes, and what LoRA freezes and trains.
  • Count LoRA's trainable parameters for a layer, and explain the $B = 0$ initialisation and the $\alpha/r$ scale.
  • Describe QLoRA's NF4 base and choose between full fine-tuning, LoRA and QLoRA.
Builds on: SFT & instruction tuning (what we're fine-tuning for), Matrices as transformations (shapes and rank) and Distributed training & mixed precision (bytes per parameter). Quantization goes deeper on 4-bit formats.

Roughly how much GPU memory does full fine-tuning of a 7B-parameter model take with AdamW in mixed precision, before counting activations?

  1. About 7 GB
  2. About 14 GB
  3. About 110 GB
  4. About 1 TB
About 16 bytes per parameter: bf16 weights (2) and gradients (2), plus an fp32 master copy (4) and Adam's two moment estimates (8). 7B × 16 bytes ≈ 112 GB, more than one 80 GB GPU holds.

A $4096 \times 4096$ matrix can be written as a product $B A$ of a $4096 \times 8$ and an $8 \times 4096$ matrix only if…

  1. its rank is at most 8
  2. it is symmetric
  3. all its entries are small
  4. any matrix can be written that way
The product of a $d \times 8$ and an $8 \times d$ matrix has rank at most 8. LoRA bets that the change a fine-tune needs is close to such a low-rank matrix.

Where full fine-tuning's memory goes

Training with AdamW in mixed precision keeps, for every parameter: the bf16 weight (2 bytes), its bf16 gradient (2), an fp32 master copy of the weight (4) and Adam's first and second moments (4 + 4). That's about 16 bytes per parameter before activations. For a 7B model: $7 \times 10^9 \times 16 \approx 112$ GB.

There's a storage cost too. Every fine-tuned variant is a full copy of the model: 14 GB in bf16 for each customer, language or task.

LoRA: learn a low-rank update

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation; Hu et al., 2021) starts from an observation: the weight change $\Delta W$ a fine-tune needs seems to have low "intrinsic rank". So instead of learning a full $\Delta W$, it learns it as a product of two thin matrices:

$$h = W_0\,x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,B A\,x, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\quad A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\quad r \ll \min(d, k)$$
  • $W_0$ ($d \times k$) is the pretrained weight. It is frozen: no gradients, no optimizer state.
  • $A$ projects the input down to $r$ numbers, and $B$ projects back up. Only $A$ and $B$ are trained.
  • $r$ is the rank, typically 8 to 64. $\alpha$ is a constant; dividing by $r$ means you can change $r$ without retuning the learning rate much. Common choices set $\alpha = r$ or $2r$.
  • Initialisation: $A$ random, $B = 0$. So $BA = 0$ and training starts from exactly the pretrained model.

The paper applied LoRA to the attention projections of GPT-3 175B and reported matching full fine-tuning on the tasks it tested, while cutting trainable parameters by about 10,000× and GPU memory by about 3×. Today it's common to put adapters on every linear layer (attention and MLP), which QLoRA found was needed to match full fine-tuning quality.

Worked example

One attention projection with $d = k = 4096$ and rank $r = 8$.

  1. Full matrix: $4096 \times 4096 = 16{,}777{,}216$ parameters
  2. A: $r \times k = 8 \times 4096 = 32{,}768$. B: $d \times r = 4096 \times 8 = 32{,}768$
  3. LoRA total: $r(d + k) = 8 \times 8192 = 65{,}536$
  4. Fraction: $65{,}536 / 16{,}777{,}216 \approx 0.39\%$, i.e. 256× fewer trainable numbers

Where the analogy breaks: a sticky note changes one page, but LoRA's $BA$ is a small change spread across the whole layer, applied to every input that passes through it.

Merging and swapping adapters

Because the adapter is just a matrix of the same shape as $W_0$, you can fold it in after training: $W' = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA$. The merged model is an ordinary model with no extra inference cost. Subtract the same term to unmerge.

Or keep adapters separate: load one base model and many small adapters, one per task or customer, and pick the adapter per request. Serving systems such as S-LoRA (Sheng et al., 2023) and vLLM's multi-LoRA support batch requests that use different adapters on the same base.

QLoRA: a 4-bit base underneath

LoRA removes the gradients and optimizer state for the base, but the frozen bf16 weights still take 2 bytes per parameter: 130 GB for a 65B model. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) stores the frozen base in 4 bits and trains bf16 LoRA adapters on top:

  • 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4): 16 levels placed at the quantiles of a normal distribution, because pretrained weights are roughly normally distributed. Weights are quantized in blocks of 64, each block with its own scale (its absolute maximum).
  • Double quantization: those per-block fp32 scales cost $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter. Quantizing the scales themselves to 8 bits cuts that to about 0.127, saving about 0.37 bits per parameter (about 3 GB on a 65B model).
  • Paged optimizers: optimizer state can spill into CPU memory during memory spikes instead of crashing the run.

In the forward and backward pass each 4-bit block is dequantized to bf16 on the fly for the matrix multiply. Gradients flow through the frozen weights into the adapters; the 4-bit weights themselves never change. The paper fine-tuned a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU and reported quality matching 16-bit fine-tuning on its benchmarks. The costs: steps are slower than plain LoRA (all that dequantizing), and merging into a 4-bit base means dequantizing, merging and either keeping 16-bit weights or re-quantizing (which adds a little error).

When to use which

  • Full fine-tuning: plenty of data and GPUs, and a big shift in what the model must know, such as continued pretraining on a large new domain or language. Biderman et al. (2024), "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less", found LoRA lags full fine-tuning in that regime, while preserving more of the base model's other skills.
  • LoRA: the default for task, style and instruction tuning; many adapters sharing one base; fast experiments. Typical starting points (tune them for your task): adapters on all linear layers, $r$ = 16, a learning rate around $10^{-4}$ to $2 \times 10^{-4}$, which is higher than full fine-tuning usually uses.
  • QLoRA: when GPU memory is the bottleneck: a big model on a small GPU. Accept slower steps and a quantized base.
Why this matters in practice: LoRA-family adapters are how most teams outside the big labs fine-tune open-weight models, through libraries such as Hugging Face PEFT (with bitsandbytes for QLoRA). Many hosted fine-tuning services also train adapters rather than full copies. And because an adapter starts as an exact no-op ($B = 0$), your first evaluation after a few steps should look like the base model; if it doesn't, check your data pipeline before blaming the method.
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Explain to a teammate why LoRA saves so much memory, and why $B$ starts at zero.

Model answer: LoRA freezes the pretrained weights, so they need no gradients or Adam state, and trains only two thin matrices $A$ ($r \times k$) and $B$ ($d \times r$) whose product is a rank-$r$ update; with $r \ll d$ that's a tiny fraction of the parameters. $B = 0$ makes $BA = 0$ at the start, so the adapted model begins exactly at the pretrained model and training only moves away from it as the data demands.
Check yourself

A $1024 \times 1024$ layer gets a LoRA adapter with $r = 4$. How many trainable parameters does the adapter add?

  1. 4,096
  2. 8,192
  3. 16,384
  4. 1,048,576
$r(d + k) = 4 \times (1024 + 1024) = 8{,}192$: $A$ is $4 \times 1024$ and $B$ is $1024 \times 4$. 1,048,576 is the full matrix; 4,096 counts only one of the two.

Why is $B$ initialised to zero (with $A$ random)?

  1. So the adapter can be merged later
  2. To make the adapter sparse and fast
  3. So $BA = 0$ and fine-tuning starts exactly from the pretrained model
  4. So that $A$ stays frozen
With $B = 0$ the bypass outputs nothing at step 0. ($A$ is random so that $B$'s gradient isn't zero; if both were zero, neither could ever learn.) Merging works whatever the initialisation.

In QLoRA, which statement is true?

  1. The frozen base is stored in 4-bit NF4 and dequantized to bf16 for each matmul; the LoRA adapters are trained in 16-bit
  2. The adapters are stored and trained in 4 bits
  3. The 4-bit base weights are updated by the optimizer
  4. It only works for models under 7B parameters
Only the adapters train, in 16-bit. The base stays frozen in 4 bits; gradients flow through it (after dequantization) to reach the adapters. The paper fine-tuned 65B models on one 48 GB GPU.

Connect to M2.5: with mixed-precision AdamW, about how many bytes per parameter does full fine-tuning need, before activations?

  1. 2
  2. 4
  3. 8
  4. 16
bf16 weights (2) + bf16 gradients (2) + fp32 master weights (4) + Adam $m$ and $v$ (4 + 4) = 16. LoRA keeps only the 2-byte frozen weights for the base; QLoRA about half a byte.

Key takeaways

  • Full fine-tuning with AdamW costs ~16 bytes per parameter; most of it is gradients and optimizer state.
  • LoRA freezes $W_0$ and learns $\Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r}BA$ with rank $r \ll d$: $r(d+k)$ parameters instead of $dk$.
  • $B = 0$ at init means training starts at the pretrained model; adapters can be merged for free inference or swapped per task.
  • QLoRA keeps the frozen base in 4-bit NF4 (with double quantization and paged optimizers) and trains 16-bit adapters on top.
  • Full FT for big knowledge shifts, LoRA by default, QLoRA when memory is the limit.
