Gradients

Lesson 4 of Math you actually need

A model has millions or billions of adjustable numbers, not one. The gradient packs "how does the loss change if I nudge each number?" into a single vector, and that vector has a beautiful property: it points straight uphill.

By the end you can

  • Compute partial derivatives by holding the other inputs fixed.
  • Assemble the gradient, and explain why it points in the steepest uphill direction, perpendicular to contour lines.
  • Connect the gradient to the gradient-descent update $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L$.
Builds on: Derivatives & the chain rule (P1.3) for slopes, and Vectors & dot products (P1.1) for $a\cdot b = \lVert a\rVert\,\lVert b\rVert\cos\theta$, which explains why the gradient is the steepest direction.

On a hiking map, contour lines join points of equal height. From where you stand, the steepest way uphill is…

  1. along the contour line
  2. always straight towards the summit
  3. at right angles to the contour line
  4. at 45° to the contour line
Along a contour you don't climb at all. The steepest climb is at right angles to it, and that's exactly the gradient's direction. (It need not point at the summit; in the last step of the animation, you'll see why that matters.)

A model has 1 billion parameters. How many numbers are in the gradient of its loss?

  1. 1 billion, one per parameter
  2. 1, the total slope
  3. 2, a direction and a length
  4. 1 billion squared
The gradient has one entry per input: "how much does the loss change if I nudge this parameter?" (The billion-squared object exists too: it's the Hessian of second derivatives, which is far too big to compute for an LLM.)

Functions of many inputs

A model's loss depends on all its parameters at once: $L(\theta_1, \theta_2, \dots, \theta_n)$. We'll build intuition with two inputs, where we can still draw a picture:

$$f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$$

Think of $f$ as the height of a landscape above the point $(x, y)$. This one is a bowl with its bottom at $(0, 0)$, steeper in the $y$ direction (because of the 3). We draw it from above like a hiking map: contour lines connect points of equal height.

Partial derivatives: one knob at a time

To ask "how does $f$ change with $x$?", freeze every other input and take an ordinary derivative. That's a partial derivative, written with a curly $\partial$:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 6y$$

For $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ the $3y^2$ term is a constant, so its derivative is 0. For $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$ the $x^2$ term is the constant. Another example, where the inputs are mixed: for $g(x, y) = x^2 y + y$, we get $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 2xy$ ($y$ is just a constant multiplier) and $\frac{\partial g}{\partial y} = x^2 + 1$.

The gradient: all the partials in one vector

$$\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2},\ \dots,\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right)$$

$\nabla$ is read "nabla" or "grad". It's a vector with one entry per input, and it lives at a point: the gradient changes as you move. Watch it get built at one point:

Why steepest, and why perpendicular?

Take a tiny step of length $\varepsilon$ in some direction $u$, a unit vector. Each coordinate moves by $\varepsilon u_i$ and contributes about $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,\varepsilon u_i$ to the change in $f$. Adding these up is a dot product:

$$\Delta f \approx \varepsilon\,(\nabla f \cdot u) = \varepsilon\,\lVert\nabla f\rVert\cos\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $u$ and $\nabla f$. The slope in direction $u$, called the directional derivative, is $\nabla f \cdot u$. From P1.1:

  • It's largest when $u$ points along $\nabla f$ ($\cos\theta = 1$): the gradient is the steepest ascent direction, with slope $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$.
  • It's zero when $u \perp \nabla f$: those directions keep $f$ constant, so they run along the contour. That's why the gradient is perpendicular to contours.
  • It's most negative for $u$ opposite to $\nabla f$: $-\nabla f$ is steepest descent.
Explain like I'm 5

You're standing on a hill in the fog with a magic compass. Instead of pointing north, its needle always points the way that goes uphill fastest. And the needle is longer when the hill is steeper.

If you walk sideways to the needle, you stay at the same height, like walking around the hill on a flat path. Want to get down fast? Walk the way the needle's tail points.

Where it breaks: a real compass works anywhere on the map, but this needle only tells the truth right where you're standing. Take a step and you have to look again.

Playground: move the point

Same bowl, $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$. Drag the sliders or tap the map. The orange arrow is $\nabla f$ (shortened to fit); the solid ellipse is the contour through your point.

Try several descent steps from $(2, 1)$. The path curves: the steep $y$ direction gets fixed quickly, while $x$ creeps in. Steepest descent heads straight downhill from where you are, not straight at the minimum.

Worked example

At $P = (1, 1)$ on $f = x^2 + 3y^2$, so $\nabla f = (2, 6)$. Compare the slope in three unit directions:

  1. Due east, $u = (1, 0)$: slope $= 2 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 0 = 2$
  2. $u = (0.6, 0.8)$: slope $= 2 \cdot 0.6 + 6 \cdot 0.8 = 6.0$
  3. Along the gradient, $u = (2, 6)/\sqrt{40}$: slope $= \lVert\nabla f\rVert = \sqrt{40} \approx 6.32$, the maximum ✓

From gradient to gradient descent

If $\nabla L$ points uphill on the loss landscape, then a small step along $-\nabla L$ lowers the loss as fast as any step of that size can. That's the whole of gradient descent, applied to every parameter at once:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L(\theta)$$

$\theta$ holds all the parameters, $\eta$ is the learning rate, and $\nabla L(\theta)$ is the gradient at the current parameters. Lesson Gradient descent (P2.2) explores the learning rate and the zig-zag you saw in the playground. The gradient itself, for a network, comes from the chain rule run backwards: Backpropagation (P2.3).

Keep in mind that the gradient is local. It describes the landscape only in a small neighbourhood of the current point. That's why we take small steps and recompute.

Why this matters for LLMs: every training step computes a gradient with exactly as many entries as the model has parameters, billions of them, then nudges each parameter against its entry. Gradients are big enough to matter for memory: with the AdamW optimizer you store, per parameter, the weight, its gradient and two running averages. And one number summarises the whole vector: the gradient norm $\lVert\nabla L\rVert$. Training runs log it, and many clip it (rescale the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold such as 1.0) to stop a single bad batch from causing a huge step.
Explain it back · in one or two sentences

Why must the gradient be perpendicular to the contour line through a point?

Model answer: The slope in a direction $u$ is $\nabla f\cdot u$. Along a contour, $f$ doesn't change, so that slope is 0, and a zero dot product means the contour direction is at right angles to $\nabla f$.
Check yourself

For $f(x, y) = x^2 y$, what is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$?

  1. $2x$
  2. $2xy$
  3. $x^2$
  4. $2xy + x^2$
Treat $y$ as a constant multiplier: $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 y) = 2x \cdot y$. ($x^2$ is $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$, and option D adds the two partials, which isn't a thing.)

At some point, $\nabla f = (3, -4)$. How steep is the steepest uphill direction there?

  1. $3$
  2. $-1$
  3. $7$
  4. $5$
The steepest slope is the gradient's length, $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, in the direction $(3, -4)/5$. The steepest way down is the opposite direction, $(-3, 4)/5$.

You walk along a contour line of $f$. The directional derivative in your walking direction is…

  1. 0
  2. $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$
  3. $-\lVert\nabla f\rVert$
  4. it depends on how fast you walk
On a contour, $f$ stays the same, so the rate of change along it is 0. Equivalently, your direction is perpendicular to $\nabla f$, so $\nabla f \cdot u = 0$.

(Uses P1.1) At $P$, $\nabla f = (2, 6)$. You step a little in the unit direction $u = (-0.6, -0.8)$. What happens to $f$?

  1. It goes up, because the gradient is positive
  2. Nothing, because you're on a contour
  3. It goes down: $\nabla f \cdot u = -1.2 - 4.8 = -6 < 0$
  4. You can't tell without $f$'s formula
The sign of the dot product says whether $u$ points with or against the gradient. Here the angle is over 90°, so it's downhill, and nearly as steep as $-\nabla f$ itself ($-6$ vs $-6.32$).

Key takeaways

  • A partial derivative $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}$ is the slope in one input with all the others held fixed.
  • The gradient $\nabla f$ stacks them: one entry per input (per parameter, for a model).
  • The slope in direction $u$ is $\nabla f \cdot u$, so $\nabla f$ is the steepest ascent, with steepness $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$.
  • $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to contour lines; $-\nabla f$ is steepest descent.
  • Gradient descent: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L$, recomputing the (local) gradient each step.
