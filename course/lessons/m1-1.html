Attention

Lesson 1 of Transformers

Attention is the one idea that makes transformers work. It lets every word look at every other word and decide which ones matter for understanding it. In this lesson you'll take it apart step by step: queries, keys, values, one softmax and one weighted sum.

You'll need

  • Vectors & dot products (P1.1): a dot product is large when two vectors point the same way.
  • Softmax (P3.2): turns any list of numbers into positive weights that sum to 1.
  • Embeddings (P3.4): each token starts life as a learned vector.

By the end you can

  • Explain what queries, keys and values are, and where they come from.
  • Compute scaled dot-product attention by hand for a tiny example.
  • Explain why scores are divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$ and what the causal mask does.
  • Say why attention's cost grows with the square of the sequence length.
Warm-up · guess before you learn

No pressure. A guess now makes the answer stick later.

In “I sat by the river bank”, how can a transformer tell that bank means a riverside, not a place for money?

  1. It looks the word up in a built-in dictionary of meanings
  2. It updates bank's vector with a weighted mix of the other words' vectors, mostly river
  3. It only ever looks at the single word right before bank
  4. It can't. Each word always has the same vector.
That weighted mix is exactly what attention computes. The weights depend on the input, so the same word gets a different vector in a different sentence.

For one word, its attention weights over the words it can see…

  1. are all positive and add up to 1
  2. are always equal
  3. can be any number, including negative
  4. are fixed after training, whatever the sentence
They come out of a softmax, so they're positive and sum to 1. And they're recomputed for every input.

The problem: words change meaning with context

A language model starts by turning each token into an embedding, a vector looked up in a table. But that table has only one vector for “bank”, whether the sentence is about rivers or mortgages. Something has to mix in context, so that “bank” next to “river” ends up with a different vector than “bank” next to “loan”.

Attention does this mixing. For each token it asks: which other tokens are relevant to me, and how much? Then it rebuilds the token's vector as a weighted blend of what those tokens offer.

Queries, keys and values

Every token's embedding $x$ is multiplied by three learned matrices to produce three new vectors:

  • Query $q = x\,W_Q$: what this token is looking for.
  • Key $k = x\,W_K$: what this token offers, the label others match against.
  • Value $v = x\,W_V$: the information it hands over if someone attends to it.

A query is compared with every key by a dot product. A large score means a good match. The scores become weights, and the weights decide how much of each value to blend in. Watch it happen for one token:

The whole thing in one line

Stack all $n$ tokens as rows of a matrix $X$. Then $Q = XW_Q$, $K = XW_K$, $V = XW_V$, and all the attention in a layer is:

$$\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}+M\right)V$$

Vaswani et al., 2017, “Attention Is All You Need”. $d_k$ is the length of each query/key vector.

  1. $QK^\top$ is an $n \times n$ table: row $i$ holds token $i$'s score against every token $j$.
  2. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps those scores in a sensible range (more below).
  3. $M$ is the causal mask: $0$ where $j \le i$ and $-\infty$ where $j > i$. Since $e^{-\infty} = 0$, future tokens get exactly zero weight. A decoder LLM generates text left to right, so it must never peek at the words it's about to predict.
  4. softmax is applied to each row, so every token's weights are positive and sum to 1.
  5. Multiplying by $V$ gives each token its new vector: a weighted average of the values.
Explain like I'm 5: query, key, value

You walk into a library with a question (the query). Every box has a label on the front (the key). The stuff inside is the value.

You compare your question with every label. Boxes whose labels match well open wide, poor matches open just a crack. You walk out with a mix of everything, mostly from the best-matching boxes.

Why divide by √dk?

Suppose the numbers inside $q$ and $k$ are roughly independent with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product adds up $d_k$ such products, so its variance is about $d_k$ and its typical size is about $\sqrt{d_k}$. With $d_k = 128$, scores of ±11 are routine.

Softmax is very sensitive to big gaps between scores: it collapses to almost one-hot, putting nearly all weight on one token. Then the gradients for every other token almost vanish, and learning stalls. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ brings the variance back to about 1, whatever the vector size.

Worked example (by hand)

Three tokens, $d_k = 2$. The third token's query is $q = [1, 2]$. The keys are $k_1 = [1,0]$, $k_2 = [0,1]$, $k_3 = [1,1]$, and the values are the same vectors, $v_i = k_i$.

  1. Scores: $q\cdot k_1 = 1$, $q\cdot k_2 = 2$, $q\cdot k_3 = 1+2 = 3$.
  2. Scale by $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$: $[0.71,\ 1.41,\ 2.12]$.
  3. Softmax: $e^{0.71} \approx 2.03$, $e^{1.41} \approx 4.11$, $e^{2.12} \approx 8.34$. The sum is $14.48$, so the weights are $[0.14,\ 0.28,\ 0.58]$.
  4. Blend the values: $0.14\,[1,0] + 0.28\,[0,1] + 0.58\,[1,1] = [0.72,\ 0.86]$

Why this matters later: every token scores every other token, so a sequence of $n$ tokens needs $n^2$ scores, in every head of every layer. At 1,000 tokens that's a million scores; at 100,000 tokens, ten billion. This quadratic cost is why KV caching (M1.5) and FlashAttention (M5.4) exist. Attention by itself also ignores word order, which is why we add positional encodings (M1.3). And real models run many attentions in parallel, which is multi-head attention, coming up next in M1.2.
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Explain to a friend what the query, key and value are for, and what the softmax does.

Model answer: Each token makes a query (what it's looking for), a key (what it offers to others) and a value (what it passes on). A token's query is dot-producted with every key to get relevance scores, and softmax turns those scores into positive weights that sum to 1. The token's new vector is the weighted average of all the values.
Check yourself

For a sequence of $n$ tokens, what is the shape of the score matrix $QK^\top$?

  1. $n \times n$
  2. $n \times d_k$
  3. $d_k \times d_k$
  4. $n \times 1$
$Q$ is $n \times d_k$ and $K^\top$ is $d_k \times n$, so the product is $n \times n$: one score for every (query, key) pair. That's the source of the quadratic cost.

Why are scores divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$?

  1. To make the computation faster
  2. To make the attention weights sum to 1
  3. To keep the scores' variance near 1, so softmax doesn't saturate and gradients keep flowing
  4. To turn query and key into unit vectors
A dot product of $d_k$ random unit-variance entries has variance $\approx d_k$. Scaling restores variance ≈ 1. Summing to 1 is softmax's job, not the scaling's.

What does the causal mask do in a decoder-only LLM?

  1. Randomly drops some attention weights during training
  2. Sets the scores for future tokens to $-\infty$, so their weights become exactly 0
  3. Removes the first token from the sequence
  4. Forces each token to attend only to itself
The model predicts the next token, so it must not see it. With $-\infty$ scores, $e^{-\infty}=0$ and the future gets no weight. Random dropping is dropout, a different thing.

Where do $W_Q$, $W_K$ and $W_V$ come from?

  1. Engineers design them by hand
  2. They are recomputed from each input sentence
  3. They are the embedding table
  4. They are learned parameters, trained by gradient descent along with the rest of the model
They're ordinary weight matrices, fixed after training. What changes with the input are the vectors $q$, $k$, $v$ they produce, and therefore the attention weights.

Key takeaways

  • Attention rebuilds each token's vector as a weighted average of values, using weights that depend on the context.
  • The weights come from a softmax over query·key scores, scaled by $1/\sqrt{d_k}$.
  • $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ are learned; $q, k, v$ and the weights change with every input.
  • Decoder LLMs use a causal mask so tokens can't see the future.
  • Cost grows as $n^2$ in sequence length, which drives a lot of later optimization work.
