Video understanding

Lesson 4 of Video

Generating video is half the story; the other half is understanding it: what is happening, when, and why. This lesson goes from classic action recognition to today's video-LLMs that answer questions about an hour-long clip, and does the token arithmetic that decides how much of the video they actually see.

By the end you can

  • Name the main video understanding tasks and the classic models behind them (two-stream networks, I3D, video transformers).
  • Explain how a video-LLM turns sampled frames into tokens an LLM can read.
  • Compute a video's token budget (frames × tokens per frame vs context length) and pick strategies for long videos.

The tasks, and the classic toolkit

Video understanding covers a family of tasks:

  • Action recognition: label a short clip ("playing guitar"). The workhorse dataset is Kinetics (Kay et al., 2017): 400 human action classes, each with at least 400 clips of about 10 seconds from YouTube.
  • Temporal localization: find when something happens: the start and end of each action in a long, untrimmed video.
  • Video question answering, captioning and retrieval: answer "why did the man go back inside?", describe the clip, or find the clip that matches a query.

Early deep models struggled with time. Karpathy et al. (2014) trained CNNs on a million sports videos and found that fusing several frames helped only a little over a single-frame model. Two-stream networks (Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014) added motion explicitly: one CNN looks at a single RGB frame (appearance), a second looks at a stack of optical flow fields (how each pixel moves between frames), and their predictions are combined.

I3D (Carreira & Zisserman, 2017), the paper that introduced Kinetics, "inflated" proven 2D image networks into 3D: each $k \times k$ filter becomes $t \times k \times k$, initialized by repeating the 2D weights over time and dividing by $t$, so the network starts out behaving like the image model and then learns motion. Later came SlowFast (Feichtenhofer et al., 2019), with a slow pathway for detail and a fast one for motion, and from 2021 the video transformers from VD.1 (TimeSformer, ViViT), pretrained at scale with methods such as VideoMAE (Tong et al., 2022), which masks out 90% or more of the space-time patches and learns to reconstruct them.

A recurring lesson: datasets where a single frame gives the answer away reward models that ignore time. Something-Something (Goyal et al., 2017) was built so that only motion distinguishes classes, such as "pushing something from left to right" versus "from right to left".

In a two-stream network, what does the second (temporal) stream look at?

  1. Stacked optical flow: how pixels move between consecutive frames
  2. The audio track
  3. The same RGB frame at a higher resolution
The spatial stream sees appearance from one frame; the temporal stream sees motion from optical flow. Combining them was a big step for action recognition in 2014.

Video-LLMs: frames in, words out

Classifiers answer a fixed question. Since 2023, video-LLMs answer any question in words. The recipe extends the vision-language model from MM.1:

  1. Sample frames: a fixed number (early systems such as Video-LLaVA, 2023, used 8) or a fixed rate such as 1 or 2 frames per second.
  2. Encode each frame with an image encoder, usually a ViT. At 336 × 336 pixels with 14-pixel patches that's $24 \times 24 = 576$ tokens per frame, as in LLaVA-1.5.
  3. Shrink the tokens (pooling or merging), project them into the LLM's embedding space, and add time information: timestamps written as text, or positions that encode real time. Qwen2.5-VL (2025), for example, samples at a dynamic frame rate and aligns its time position encoding with absolute time.
  4. Put the frame tokens before the question and let the LLM generate the answer.

Some models encode short groups of frames together, with space-time patches like the tubelets in VD.1, which captures motion better and halves the tokens. Others, like Google's Gemini models (since late 2023), are trained natively on interleaved video, audio and text and accept very long inputs. Either way, the LLM only "sees" what survives sampling and compression.

Explain like I'm 5

Your friend watched a whole movie, but you only have time to look at a stack of photos from it. If someone took one photo every few seconds, you can tell a pretty good story: who was there, where they went, what happened at the end.

But if the photos are far apart, you might miss the moment the cat knocked over the vase. And if each photo is tiny and blurry so more of them fit in your hands, you might miss the vase altogether. A video-LLM juggles exactly that: how many photos, and how sharp.

Where it breaks: a person flipping through photos remembers earlier ones loosely; a video-LLM reads every token in its context exactly, but can't see anything that never made it into the context.

Why does a video-LLM need explicit time information (timestamps or time-aware positions) for sampled frames?

  1. To make the frames load faster
  2. Because the image encoder needs it to recognize objects
  3. Frames may be sampled at varying rates, so their order alone doesn't say when, or how far apart, events happened
"At 12:40" or "two seconds later" needs real time. With dynamic or sparse sampling, frame index ≠ time, so models add timestamps or absolute-time positions.

The token budget problem

Everything a video-LLM sees must fit in its context window, alongside the question and the answer. The budget is simple multiplication:

$$\text{tokens} = \underbrace{\text{duration} \times \text{sampling rate}}_{\text{frames}} \times \text{tokens per frame}$$

Worked example: a 1-hour video, sampled at 1 frame per second.

  1. Frames: $3{,}600 \times 1 = 3{,}600$.
  2. At 576 tokens per frame: $3{,}600 \times 576 = 2{,}073{,}600$ tokens, about 16× a 128K-token context.
  3. Pool 2 × 2 to 144 tokens per frame: $518{,}400$ tokens, still about 4×.
  4. For comparison, Google's Gemini API documentation (as of 2025–26) describes sampling video at 1 frame per second with about 258 tokens per frame at default resolution: roughly 930,000 tokens for an hour of frames, plus audio. That fits only because those models accept around a million tokens or more.

Long videos, and how we test them

When the budget doesn't fit, systems trade off three things: when they look, how sharply, and what they remember.

  • Smarter sampling: sparser uniform sampling is cheap but misses short events. Alternatives pick frames at shot changes, or score frames for relevance to the question (for example with a CLIP-style text-image similarity) and keep the best.
  • Fewer tokens per frame: spatial pooling, or token merging (ToMe, Bolya et al., 2023), which merges similar tokens. Consecutive frames of a static scene are near-duplicates, so merging across time saves a lot.
  • Memory: stream the video, compress older frames into a fixed-size memory (MovieChat, Song et al., 2024, is one example), or caption segments first and retrieve only the relevant ones.
  • Look again: agent-style systems skim at low frame rate, then "zoom in" by re-sampling densely around the moments that matter for the question.
  • Longer context: long-context models (Gemini 1.5, 2024, was an early example) simply accept around a million tokens, at a correspondingly higher compute cost.

Benchmarks. Kinetics and Something-Something test recognition; NExT-QA (Xiao et al., 2021) asks causal and temporal questions; EgoSchema (Mangalam et al., 2023) uses 3-minute first-person clips; MVBench (Li et al., 2024) covers many short temporal skills; and Video-MME (Fu et al., 2024) has 900 videos from about 11 seconds to an hour long with 2,700 multiple-choice questions, tested with and without subtitles. Long-video suites such as LongVideoBench (Wu et al., 2024) push further.

Read results with care. Many questions can be answered from one frame, the subtitles or general knowledge, and a model that ignores most of the video can still score well. Good benchmarks filter such questions (Video-MME's authors rewrote or dropped questions that a text-only model could answer) and report results by video length; good evaluations include a "blind" or single-frame baseline.

Why this matters for LLM practice: a video-LLM is an LLM with a context-budget problem, the same one you meet with long documents in retrieval-augmented generation. Sampling is chunking, token merging is compression, segment captions plus retrieval is RAG over a video, and "look again" is an agent with a tool. If a product claims to "watch" an hour of video, ask how many frames per second it samples and how many tokens each frame gets.

A video-LLM scores well on a video QA benchmark. Which extra check best tells you whether it actually used the video's temporal content?

  1. Run it twice with the same input
  2. Check that its answers are long and detailed
  3. Compare with blind (text-only) and single-frame baselines on the same questions
If a text-only or single-frame model scores nearly as well, the benchmark (or the model) isn't exercising temporal understanding.
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Explain to a product manager why "our model can watch a one-hour video" needs a footnote.

Model answer: A video-LLM reads sampled frames, each turned into hundreds of tokens, and everything must fit in its context window: an hour at 1 frame per second and 576 tokens per frame is about 2 million tokens. So it either samples sparsely (and can miss short events), compresses each frame (and loses detail), or uses memory and retrieval (and may skip parts). How many frames it samples and how many tokens each gets decides what it can actually notice.
Check yourself

What did I3D (2017) do to build a video network?

  1. Replaced convolutions with self-attention over frames
  2. Inflated a 2D image network's filters into 3D (adding a time dimension), initialized from the 2D weights
  3. Used only optical flow, discarding RGB frames
  4. Generated videos with a GAN
I3D reused proven image architectures and ImageNet weights by repeating each $k \times k$ filter over $t$ frames (divided by $t$), then trained on Kinetics. Attention-only video models came in 2021.

Interleaving with V1.1: a ViT encodes a 448 × 448 frame with 14-pixel patches. How many tokens per frame, before any pooling?

  1. 576
  2. 784
  3. 196
  4. 1,024
$448 / 14 = 32$ patches per side, so $32^2 = 1{,}024$ tokens. Higher resolution per frame eats the frame budget quickly.

A video-LLM samples 1 frame every 30 seconds to fit a 2-hour film into its context. Which question is it most likely to get wrong?

  1. "What did the man pick up just before the door slammed?"
  2. "Is this film set in a city or the countryside?"
  3. "Roughly how many main characters are there?"
  4. "Is it day or night in the final scene?"
Brief, fine-grained events fall between sparse samples. Global questions about setting and characters survive sparse sampling well.

Interleaving with MM.1: what does the projector in a video-LLM do?

  1. Chooses which frames to sample
  2. Converts the LLM's answer into speech
  3. Maps the vision encoder's frame tokens into the LLM's embedding space so they can sit in the same sequence as text tokens
  4. Decodes tokens back into video frames
Same role as for images: a small learned layer (often an MLP) translates visual features into "words" the LLM can read.

Key takeaways

  • Core tasks: action recognition (Kinetics), temporal localization, video QA, captioning and retrieval.
  • Classic models added motion explicitly (two-stream optical flow) or through 3D filters (I3D); video transformers and VideoMAE-style pretraining followed.
  • Video-LLMs sample frames, encode, compress, project into an LLM, with timestamps.
  • Budget: frames × tokens per frame must fit the context; 1 h at 1 fps × 576 ≈ 2.1M tokens.
  • Long videos need trade-offs (sparser or smarter sampling, merging, memory, look-again), and benchmarks such as Video-MME need blind baselines to be meaningful.
