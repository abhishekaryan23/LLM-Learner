The LLM wiki: knowledge that compounds

Lesson 6 of The agent toolkit

Retrieval looks things up from scratch every time you ask. An LLM wiki does the reading once: the model writes and keeps up a small folder of linked notes from your sources, and every new question starts from what it already worked out.

You ask a plain RAG system the same tricky question about 30 papers on Monday and again on Friday. What does Friday's answer reuse from Monday's work?

  1. Nothing: it retrieves chunks and works the answer out again
  2. Monday's answer, word for word
  3. A summary of all 30 papers it wrote on Monday
  4. The model's weights, updated on Monday
Plain RAG keeps no record of its own reasoning. Each question starts again from raw chunks. That is exactly the gap the wiki pattern fills.

In the LLM wiki pattern, who writes most of the pages?

  1. The human, by hand
  2. Nobody: the pages are the raw documents
  3. The LLM; the human picks the sources and asks the questions
  4. A search engine
The whole point is that the LLM does the boring upkeep (summaries, links, consistency) while you curate and ask.

Why personal wikis die, and the fix

The dream is old. In 1945 Vannevar Bush described the Memex, a desk that would store everything you read and tie related items together into "trails". In 1995 Ward Cunningham launched the first wiki: pages anyone can edit, connected by links. Since then countless people have started personal and team wikis, and most were quietly abandoned.

They don't die because reading or thinking is hard. They die because upkeep is boring: every new document means updating five old pages, fixing links and noticing that two pages now disagree. As Andrej Karpathy put it in an April 2026 note on GitHub, "Humans abandon wikis because the maintenance burden grows faster than the value." His note, titled LLM Wiki, proposes handing that upkeep to an LLM agent. It is deliberately an "idea file": you give it to your agent, and the two of you build a version that fits your topic.

The pattern has three layers:

  1. Raw sources (raw/): the articles, papers, transcripts and notes you collect. They are immutable: "the LLM reads from them but never modifies them".
  2. The wiki (wiki/): Markdown pages the LLM writes and owns: one page per source, pages for each concept, person or method, comparisons, an overview. Two special files: index.md, a catalogue the LLM reads first, and log.md, a running record of every change.
  3. The schema: a rules file (a CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md, exactly the kind from AG.5) that says how pages are named, how links and citations look and what to do on each operation. You and the LLM refine it over time.

You read the wiki; the LLM writes it. A Markdown viewer with a graph view (Karpathy uses Obsidian) and git for history are all the tooling you need to start.

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a big messy pile of loose papers. Every time you want to know something, you dig through the whole pile again. Tiring!

Now a librarian robot helps. Each time a new paper arrives, it reads it, glues a neat page about it into a scrapbook, and ties coloured strings from the new page to the older pages it's related to.

When you have a question, the robot opens the scrapbook, not the pile. The scrapbook gets better with every paper, and the original papers stay safe in their box.

Where it breaks: a real scrapbook never changes its old pages. The LLM keeps rewriting older pages as it learns more, and if it misreads a paper, the mistake can spread along the strings. That's why you still check its work.

In the LLM wiki pattern, what may the LLM never do?

  1. Rewrite an older wiki page
  2. Edit the raw sources
  3. Add links between pages
  4. Append to log.md
Raw sources are the ground truth and stay immutable. Everything in wiki/ can be rewritten as understanding improves, because it can always be checked against raw/.

Ingest, query, lint

Three operations keep the wiki alive. Step through one round of each:

  • Ingest. Add one source. The LLM reads it, writes a page about it, and updates every related page: new facts, new links, notes where the new source disagrees with an old one. Then it updates index.md and appends to log.md. Karpathy prefers ingesting one source at a time and skimming the changes, so you stay involved.
  • Query. Ask a question. The LLM reads the index, opens the relevant pages and answers with citations. Good answers (a comparison, an analysis) can be filed back as new pages, so the next question builds on them.
  • Lint. Now and then, ask for a health check: contradictions between pages, claims that newer sources have made stale, orphan pages nobody links to, missing links, and gaps worth a new source.

How big can it get? The note says the index-file approach works "surprisingly well at moderate scale (~100 sources, ~hundreds of pages)" with no embeddings or vector database. Past that, the LLM can no longer skim the whole index in one go, and you add a search tool. The note mentions qmd, a local search engine for Markdown files that can also run as an MCP server (AG.3). At that point you are mixing in retrieval again, which is fine: the two ideas combine.

Why does the LLM read index.md first when answering a question?

  1. It is the only file it is allowed to read
  2. It contains the raw sources
  3. It is a short catalogue of every page, so the LLM can pick which pages to open without loading the whole wiki
  4. It stores the model's weights
The index plays the part that a vector search plays in RAG: it points to the few pages worth reading, which keeps the context window small (AG.2).

Why it compounds, and where it fits

With plain RAG, the hard work of reading, connecting and reconciling sources happens at question time, and is thrown away after the answer. With a wiki, it happens at ingest time and is kept. Cross-references already exist. Contradictions have already been flagged. A comparison you asked for last week is now a page. Each source and each good question makes the next answer cheaper and better: the knowledge compounds.

That trade has a price. Ingest is expensive (one source can touch 10 to 15 pages), and everything rests on the summaries being right. So the wiki shines on a small, slowly growing body of knowledge you return to often:

  • Personal research: the 40 papers you are reading on one topic, with pages per method, dataset and author.
  • A team's design notes: decisions, meeting notes and the reasons behind them, cross-linked by component.
  • Onboarding: how a codebase or a business really works, pieced together from scattered docs.
  • A reading companion for a long book, or a due-diligence file on one company.

It fits badly when the corpus is huge (millions of support tickets), fast-changing (prices, stock levels, live incidents: query those directly with a tool), or when you need exact wording and an audit trail (legal or medical answers that must quote the original). In those cases use RAG or direct tool calls over the raw data.

Which job fits an LLM wiki best?

  1. Answering questions over 3 million customer support tickets
  2. Showing the live stock level of every product
  3. Quoting the exact clause of a contract in a legal dispute
  4. Building up your understanding of 50 papers on one research question over a month
A small, slowly growing set you return to often is the sweet spot. Huge or live data calls for RAG or direct queries; exact legal wording calls for the original text.

Keeping a wiki honest

A wiki written by an LLM inherits the LLM's mistakes. The main risks, and the habits that contain them:

RiskWhat goes wrongHabit that helps
Errors baked inA misread number at ingest becomes a "fact", spreads along the links, and later answers repeat it confidently.Link every claim to its raw source, so any statement can be checked in one click. Keep raw/ read-only.
Drift and stalenessOld pages say one thing, newer sources another.Lint regularly; when sources disagree, keep both claims and mark the conflict instead of silently picking one.
Silent rewritesAn ingest quietly changes ten pages you trusted.Keep the wiki in git and skim the diff after each ingest, like a code review. log.md says what changed and why.
Planted instructionsA source contains text such as "ignore your rules and…", and the agent obeys while reading it.Treat sources as data, not instructions; say so in the schema; review diffs (see SY.7).
Scale and costThe index outgrows the context window; ingests get expensive.Add a search tool past a few hundred pages; ingest one source at a time.

Why this matters in practice: the same habits that make RAG trustworthy (cite sources, keep originals, check outputs) make a wiki trustworthy. The difference is that a wiki's mistakes are persistent: a bad RAG answer disappears after one chat, while a bad wiki page keeps answering. Citations plus lint plus a quick human skim of each change are what turn "the AI wrote my notes" into notes you can rely on.

Two of your wiki pages give different launch years for the same product. What should a good lint step do?

  1. Flag the conflict, point to both raw sources, and let you (or a re-read of the sources) settle it
  2. Pick the more recent year and silently update both pages
  3. Delete both pages
  4. Ignore it: small differences don't matter
Contradictions are exactly what lint is for. Silently picking one bakes in a possible error; pointing back to the raw sources keeps the wiki checkable.
Explain it back · in one or two sentences

What does it mean that an LLM wiki "compounds", and why doesn't plain RAG?

Model answer: In a wiki, the work of reading, linking and reconciling sources is done once at ingest and kept as pages, and good answers are filed back as new pages, so each source and question makes the next answer better. Plain RAG redoes that work from raw chunks for every question and keeps nothing afterwards.
Check yourself

What is the "schema" layer of an LLM wiki?

  1. A database table definition
  2. A rules file (like AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md) that tells the LLM how pages, links, citations and operations work
  3. The vector index used for search
  4. The folder of raw sources
It is the same kind of file as in AG.5: plain Markdown instructions loaded every session, here describing the wiki's conventions and workflows.

Your wiki has grown to 3,000 pages and answers are getting worse. What is the most likely cause, and fix?

  1. The raw sources expired; delete them
  2. The model forgot the wiki; fine-tune it on the pages
  3. The index no longer fits comfortably in the context window; add a search tool so the LLM retrieves the right pages
  4. Too many links; remove all links
The plain-index approach works at around a hundred sources and hundreds of pages. Beyond that, finding the right pages becomes a retrieval problem again (SY.3).

RAG splits documents into chunks before indexing them (SY.3). Which weakness does that create that a wiki tries to fix?

  1. An answer that needs facts spread across many documents must be pieced together from separate chunks every time
  2. Chunks make the model's weights larger
  3. Chunks can't be searched
  4. Chunking deletes the original documents
Retrieval returns a handful of similar chunks; connecting ideas across many sources is redone per question. A wiki stores those connections as pages and links.

Which habit best protects a wiki against a misread fact at ingest?

  1. Ingesting 100 sources at once to average out errors
  2. Never rereading the wiki
  3. Letting the LLM edit the raw sources to match the wiki
  4. Linking every claim to its raw source and skimming each ingest's changes
Citations make every statement checkable, and a quick diff review catches mistakes before they spread. Batch ingests make review harder, and raw sources must stay untouched.

Key takeaways

  • An LLM wiki (Karpathy, April 2026): immutable raw sources, an LLM-written folder of linked pages, and a schema rules file.
  • Three operations: ingest (summarize and update related pages), query (answer from pages, with citations, and file good answers back), lint (find contradictions and gaps).
  • It compounds because synthesis is done once and kept; RAG redoes it every question.
  • Best for small, evolving topics you revisit; use RAG or tools for huge, live or audit-critical data.
  • Keep it honest: cite raw sources, lint, review diffs, treat sources as data.
