Gradient descent

Needs: what a derivative is

Every model in this course learns the same way, from a two-parameter line to a trillion-parameter LLM: feel the slope, take a small step downhill, repeat. That loop is gradient descent.

By the end you can

  • Explain what a loss function is and why training means minimising it.
  • Compute one gradient-descent step by hand.
  • Predict what a too-small, good or too-large learning rate will do.
  • Describe the gradient in several dimensions, and why we use mini-batches.
Warm-up · guess before you learn

Take your best guess. Being wrong now helps the right answer stick later.

Nudging a parameter up a little makes the model's error go up. To reduce the error you should…

  1. Increase the parameter even more
  2. Decrease the parameter a little
  3. Leave it alone, since error can't be reduced that way
  4. Set the parameter to zero
If "up" makes things worse, "down" makes them better, at least for a small step. That one idea is the whole algorithm.

What usually happens if the step size ("learning rate") is far too large?

  1. Training is slow but very stable
  2. The loss bounces around or blows up
  3. The model memorises the training data
  4. Nothing. Step size doesn't matter.
Big steps overshoot the bottom of the valley, land higher on the other side, and can keep getting worse. You'll make this happen yourself in the playground below.

Training = finding the bottom of a landscape

A model is a function with adjustable numbers inside it, its parameters. We'll call them $\theta$ (theta). A loss function $L(\theta)$ scores how wrong the model is on the training data for a given setting of $\theta$: lower is better. Training means finding the $\theta$ that makes $L$ as small as possible.

With one parameter the loss is a curve; with two it's a landscape of hills and valleys; with an LLM it's a "landscape" in billions of dimensions. We can't look at all of it. We can only evaluate the loss, and its slope, right where we are.

Rusty on derivatives? Lesson P1.3 Derivatives & the chain rule (coming soon) covers them. For now: the derivative $\frac{dL}{d\theta}$ is the slope of the curve at a point.

The slope points uphill, so step the other way

If the slope $\frac{dL}{d\theta}$ is positive, increasing $\theta$ increases the loss, so we decrease $\theta$. If it's negative, we increase $\theta$. Both cases are one rule:

$$\theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta \;-\; \eta\,\frac{dL}{d\theta}$$

$\eta$ (eta) is the learning rate, a small positive number that scales the step. Watch it work:

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you're on a hill in thick fog, wearing a blindfold, and your house is at the very bottom of the valley.

You can't see the house. But you can feel which way the ground tilts under your feet. So you take a small step downhill, feel again, step again… and eventually you're home.

Feeling the tilt = the slope. The size of your step = the learning rate.

Worked example

Loss $L(\theta) = \theta^2$, so the slope is $\frac{dL}{d\theta} = 2\theta$. Start at $\theta = 2$ with $\eta = 0.1$.

  1. Slope: $2 \times 2 = 4$
  2. Step: $-\eta \times \text{slope} = -0.1 \times 4 = -0.4$
  3. New $\theta$: $2 - 0.4 = 1.6$
  4. Loss went from $2^2 = 4$ down to $1.6^2 = 2.56$ ✓

The learning rate: the most important knob

For $L = \theta^2$ the update simplifies to $\theta \leftarrow (1 - 2\eta)\,\theta$. Each step multiplies the distance to the bottom by $(1 - 2\eta)$, which gives four regimes:

  • Too small ($\eta$ near 0): the factor is close to 1, so progress crawls.
  • Good ($0 < \eta < 0.5$): the factor is between 0 and 1, a smooth slide to the bottom.
  • Overshooting ($0.5 < \eta < 1$): the factor is negative, so $\theta$ hops from side to side but still shrinks.
  • Diverging ($\eta > 1$): the factor's size exceeds 1, so every hop lands higher. Training explodes.

More than one parameter: the gradient

With many parameters, we take the slope in each direction separately: the partial derivatives. Stacked into a vector, they form the gradient:

$$\nabla L = \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_1},\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_2},\; \dots,\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_n}\right)$$

and the update is the same rule, applied to every parameter at once:

$$\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta - \eta\,\nabla L$$

The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and it is always perpendicular to the contour lines (lines of equal loss). So $-\nabla L$ is steepest descent. But "steepest downhill" is not the same as "straight toward the minimum":

Stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent

The loss is an average over the training data: $L(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell(\theta; x_i)$. Computing the exact gradient means a pass over all $N$ examples, which is hopeless when $N$ is trillions of tokens.

Instead, each step uses a small random mini-batch of $B$ examples and follows its gradient. That's an unbiased but noisy estimate of the true gradient. It's far cheaper, so you can take vastly more steps, and the noise even helps the optimizer escape bad flat regions. One full pass through the data is an epoch. The extreme case $B = 1$ is classic stochastic gradient descent (SGD); in practice "SGD" is often used for the mini-batch version too.

Why this matters for LLMs: pretraining an LLM is mini-batch gradient descent on the next-token loss, over billions of parameters, with batches of millions of tokens. The gradient comes from backpropagation (P2.3). The step rule is usually AdamW (M2.3), a smarter variant that adapts the step per parameter, but it is still "go against the gradient". And the learning rate, with its warm-up and decay schedule, is one of the most carefully tuned numbers in the whole training run.
Explain it back · in one or two sentences

In the animation, the learning rate never changed, yet the steps got smaller near the bottom. Why?

Model answer: The step is $\eta$ times the slope, and the slope shrinks toward zero as you approach the minimum. So even with a fixed $\eta$, the steps shrink automatically and the parameter settles at the bottom.
Check yourself

Which is the gradient-descent update rule?

  1. $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta\,\nabla L$
  2. $\theta \leftarrow \eta\,\nabla L$
  3. $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L$
  4. $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \nabla L / \eta$
Subtract the gradient (it points uphill), scaled by the learning rate. Adding it would be gradient ascent.

On $L(\theta) = \theta^2$ with learning rate $\eta = 0.75$, what does gradient descent do?

  1. Converges smoothly from one side
  2. Hops from side to side, but still converges
  3. Bounces between the same two points forever
  4. Diverges
The update is $\theta \leftarrow (1 - 2 \times 0.75)\,\theta = -0.5\,\theta$: the sign flips each step (hopping) while the size halves (converging).

With two or more parameters, the negative gradient $-\nabla L$ points…

  1. Steepest downhill, perpendicular to the contour line you're on
  2. Straight at the minimum, always
  3. Along the contour line, keeping the loss constant
  4. In a random direction that's correct on average
The gradient is perpendicular to contours. In a stretched valley that's often not toward the minimum, hence the zig-zag.

Why do we train on mini-batches rather than the full dataset each step?

  1. Mini-batch gradients are more accurate
  2. Backpropagation only works on small batches
  3. It completely prevents overfitting
  4. Each step is far cheaper, so we can take many more steps; the noisy estimate is good enough
A mini-batch gives an unbiased, noisy estimate of the full gradient at a tiny fraction of the cost. Many cheap, slightly noisy steps beat a few perfect, expensive ones.

Key takeaways

  • Training = minimising a loss $L(\theta)$ over the model's parameters.
  • The gradient points uphill; step against it: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L$.
  • The learning rate $\eta$ decides between crawling, converging, oscillating and diverging.
  • In many dimensions, $-\nabla L$ is steepest descent, perpendicular to contours, so narrow valleys cause zig-zags.
  • Mini-batch gradients are cheap, noisy estimates. Every LLM is trained this way.
