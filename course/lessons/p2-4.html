Overfitting & data splits

Lesson 4 of Machine-learning basics

A model can score perfectly on its training data and still fail on everything new. The only way to know is to test it on data it has never seen, and to guard that data carefully.

By the end you can

  • Diagnose underfitting and overfitting from training and validation error.
  • Explain what the training, validation and test splits are each for, and why the test set is used only once.
  • Describe weight decay, dropout, early stopping and more data, and how LLM training uses them.

Warm-up · guess before you learn

Take your best guess. Being wrong now helps the right answer stick later.

A model scores 99% on its training data but 60% on new examples. What's the most likely story?

  1. The model is too simple
  2. The learning rate is too small
  3. It memorised the training examples instead of learning the general pattern
  4. The new examples are mislabelled
A big gap between training and new-data performance is the signature of overfitting. A model that's too simple would do badly on both.

You try 50 hyperparameter settings and report the best score on your test set. Is that score a fair estimate of performance on new data?

  1. Yes, the test set was never trained on
  2. No, it's optimistic: you picked the setting that happened to do best on that particular test set
  3. No, it's pessimistic: test sets are always harder
  4. Yes, as long as the test set is large
Choosing among many options using the test set lets its quirks leak into your choice. That's what the separate validation set is for.

The goal is new data

We train on a fixed dataset, but we care about examples the model has never seen: tomorrow's users, the next sentence. Doing well on those is called generalisation. The training loss can't measure it, because the model was optimised on exactly those examples. A flexible enough model can drive the training loss to zero just by memorising them.

So we hold some data back. The difference between the error on held-out data and on training data is the generalisation gap. A small gap means what the model learned transfers; a big one means it learned the training set's quirks.

Three splits, three jobs

  • Training set: the only data gradient descent sees. It fits the parameters.
  • Validation set (or dev set): used to make decisions: model size, learning rate, how long to train, which checkpoint to keep. You'll look at it many times.
  • Test set: locked away and used once, at the end, to report how well the final model does on new data.

Why the extra split? Every decision you make by looking at a dataset tunes your model to that dataset a little. Try 50 settings and keep the best validation score, and part of that "best" is luck on those particular examples. The validation score becomes optimistic. The test set stays honest only if nothing was chosen with it. For small datasets a split like 80/10/10 is common; with millions of examples, a few thousand held-out ones are usually plenty.

The fits are ordinary least-squares polynomials, $\hat y = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x + \dots + \theta_d x^d$, fitted to the 10 training points only. "Degree" is the model's flexibility. Weight decay here is a penalty $\lambda \sum_j \theta_j^2$ added to the training MSE, with $\lambda = 10^{-4}$.

Explain like I'm 5

Two kids study for a test with the same practice sheet. One memorises every answer on the sheet. The other works out why the answers are right.

On test day the questions are new. The memoriser panics: none of the answers they learned fit. The kid who understood calmly solves them. Both were perfect on the practice sheet; only one learned the real thing.

That's why teachers don't put the practice questions on the real test. Where the analogy breaks: a model doesn't choose to memorise; it simply has enough flexibility to fit the noise, unless we stop it.

Reading the two curves

Compare training and validation error and you can usually tell which problem you have:

  • Underfitting: training error is high and validation error about the same. The model is too simple or hasn't trained long enough. Fixes: a bigger model, better features, more training.
  • Overfitting: training error is low but validation error is much higher, and often rising while training error still falls. Fixes: more data, regularization, a smaller model, or stopping earlier.
  • A good fit: both are low and close together.

The same U-shape appears over training time: as a flexible model trains longer, validation loss usually falls, flattens, and eventually starts rising as the model begins to fit noise.

Regularization: making memorising harder

Regularization is anything that nudges a model toward simpler solutions that generalise. The main tools:

  • Weight decay. Add $\lambda\lVert\theta\rVert^2 = \lambda\sum_j \theta_j^2$ to the loss. Its gradient is $2\lambda\theta$, so every step also pulls each weight a little toward zero, in proportion to its size. Wild fits need huge coefficients, so they become expensive. LLMs use the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), which applies the shrinkage directly to the weights instead of through the gradient (M2.3); a weight decay of 0.1 appears in many published recipes, such as GPT-3 and Llama 2.
  • Dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014). During training, randomly zero each activation with probability $p$ and scale the survivors by $\frac{1}{1-p}$, so no unit can rely on any particular other one. At evaluation time it's switched off. Large LLM pretraining runs often set $p = 0$, since they rarely see the same text twice; it's more common when fine-tuning on small datasets.
  • Early stopping. Track the validation loss during training and keep the checkpoint where it was lowest.
  • More data. The most reliable fix of all: it's much harder to memorise a million examples than ten. Data augmentation (sensible variations of existing examples) is a cheap version of this.

Worked example: one step with weight decay

A weight $\theta = 2$, gradient of the data loss $0.5$, learning rate $\eta = 0.1$, penalty $\lambda\theta^2$ with $\lambda = 0.01$.

  1. Gradient of the penalty: $2\lambda\theta = 2 \times 0.01 \times 2 = 0.04$.
  2. Total gradient: $0.5 + 0.04 = 0.54$.
  3. Update: $\theta \leftarrow 2 - 0.1 \times 0.54 = 1.946$. Without decay it would be $1.95$: the extra $-0.004$ is the pull toward zero.

Why held-out data is sacred for LLM evals

For an LLM, a benchmark (M4.2) is the test set. Its promise is "this measures performance on questions the model hasn't seen". Two things break that promise:

  • Contamination (M4.3). Pretraining data is scraped from the web, and benchmark questions, answers and discussions end up on the web. If they leak into training, a high score can partly reflect memorisation: the overfitted student who saw the exam in advance.
  • Selecting on the test set. When many models, prompts or checkpoints are compared, tuned and re-run on the same public benchmark, the field slowly overfits to it, exactly like trying 50 settings on your test set.

Good practice mirrors this lesson: keep private or freshly written test sets, check training data for overlap with benchmarks, prefer questions written after the model's training cutoff, and make decisions on a validation set rather than the reported one.

Why this matters for LLMs: pretraining is unusual. With trillions of tokens, most data is seen about once, so the training loss on the next batch is effectively a validation loss, and classic overfitting is rare. Repetition is where it creeps back: Muennighoff et al. (2023) found that repeating data for up to about 4 epochs was almost as good as fresh data, with rapidly diminishing returns beyond that. Fine-tuning (M3.1) is the opposite: small datasets, a few epochs, and validation loss watched closely, because overfitting arrives fast.
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Why do we need a validation set and a test set? Wouldn't one held-out set do?

Model answer: We make many decisions (hyperparameters, model size, when to stop) by comparing scores on held-out data, and each choice fits that data a little, so its score becomes optimistic. The validation set absorbs those decisions; the test set is used once at the end, so it still gives an honest estimate of performance on truly new data.
Check yourself

Training loss is still falling, but validation loss has risen for the last three evaluations. What's the sensible move?

  1. Train longer; validation loss always comes back down
  2. Raise the learning rate
  3. Check the test set to decide
  4. Stop, and keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss (early stopping), or add regularization
Training loss falling while validation loss rises is the classic overfitting signal. Peeking at the test set to decide would turn it into a second validation set.

Which split should you use to choose the learning rate?

  1. The training set
  2. The validation set
  3. The test set
  4. Any of them; it doesn't matter
Hyperparameters are decisions, and decisions go on the validation set. The training loss would reward whatever memorises best, and using the test set would make your final number optimistic.

A model's score on a public benchmark jumps after a new pretraining crawl, and many of the benchmark's questions turn out to be in that crawl. The new score…

  1. probably overstates how well the model handles genuinely new questions
  2. is more trustworthy, because the model has seen more data
  3. is unaffected; benchmarks measure reasoning, not memory
  4. is lower than the true ability
That's test-set contamination: the "test" data was effectively training data. More in M4.3.

Weight decay adds $\lambda\lVert\theta\rVert^2$ to the loss. In the gradient-descent update $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L$ (P2.2), what does this term do?

  1. Adds a constant $\eta\lambda$ to every weight
  2. Multiplies the learning rate by $\lambda$
  3. Adds $2\lambda\theta$ to the gradient, pulling each weight toward zero in proportion to its size
  4. Randomly sets some weights to zero
The derivative of $\lambda\theta^2$ is $2\lambda\theta$, so big weights are pulled back harder. Randomly zeroing things is dropout, and that acts on activations, not weights.

Key takeaways

  • Training error can't measure generalisation; only data the model never trained on can.
  • Train fits parameters, validation makes decisions, test is used once to report.
  • Underfit: both errors high. Overfit: training error low, validation error much higher.
  • Regularize with weight decay, dropout, early stopping and, best of all, more data.
  • LLM benchmarks are test sets: contamination and repeated selection quietly inflate scores.
