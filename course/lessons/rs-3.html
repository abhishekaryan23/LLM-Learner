Training reasoning models

Lesson 3 of Reasoning & test-time compute

Prompting a model to "think step by step" only uses whatever reasoning skill it already has. Since 2022, and dramatically since late 2024, labs train that skill: the model writes long chains of thought, a program checks the final answer, and reinforcement learning makes the habits that lead to right answers more likely. The result is the "reasoning model".

  • Estimate what long reasoning costs at inference and in RL training, and how thinking budgets control it.

A model writes rationales for 10,000 maths questions with known answers. You want to fine-tune it on its own rationales. Which ones should you keep?

  1. Those whose final answer matches the known answer
  2. All of them, since more data is better
  3. The longest ones
  4. Those the model was most confident about
That filter is the heart of STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022): rationales that reach the right answer are probably (not certainly) good reasoning, so the model learns from its own successes.

DeepSeek-R1-Zero learned long, self-checking reasoning through reinforcement learning. What produced its reward?

  1. A neural reward model trained on human preferences
  2. Human raters scoring every response
  3. Simple rules: is the final answer correct, and is the reasoning in the required format?
  4. A process reward model scoring every step
Rule-based accuracy and format rewards only. The authors avoided neural reward models for this stage, partly because of reward hacking (RS.4).

From prompting to training: STaR

Chain-of-thought prompting (RS.1, 2022) showed that large models can reason step by step, but the prompt doesn't make them any better at it. Training on worked solutions helps (that's just SFT, M3.1), yet good human-written rationales are slow and expensive to collect. Could the model write its own training data?

STaR, the Self-Taught Reasoner (Zelikman et al., 2022), said yes, with a simple loop:

  1. Generate: with a few worked examples in the prompt, the model writes a rationale and an answer for every training question.
  2. Filter: keep only the rationales whose final answer is correct.
  3. Rationalize: for questions it got wrong, show it the correct answer as a hint and ask for a rationale that reaches it. Keep those that do. This gives signal on questions the model can't yet solve on its own.
  4. Fine-tune on the kept rationales, then repeat with the improved model.

On CommonsenseQA, STaR clearly beat fine-tuning to predict answers directly, and performed about as well as a model roughly 30× larger fine-tuned on the task. The same idea returns under other names: rejection-sampling fine-tuning (Yuan et al., 2023) and ReST-EM (Singh et al., 2023), which frames it as expectation-maximization.

Seen from RL, STaR is a policy-gradient method with a 0/1 reward that only learns from successes: it raises the probability of rationales with reward 1, and does nothing with the rest. It never says "this attempt was worse than average", and it learns from batches of old samples rather than from what the current model does now. Those are the two gaps reinforcement learning fills.

A filter on the final answer keeps some lucky rationales with wrong steps. RS.4 is about that problem.

What is STaR's "rationalization" step for?

  1. Shortening rationales to save tokens
  2. Getting training rationales for questions the model failed, by giving it the correct answer as a hint
  3. Checking each step with a process reward model
  4. Translating rationales into other languages
Without it, the model only learns from questions it already solves. With the answer as a hint, it can write a rationale that reaches it, which is kept only if it does.

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

A quick recap of the RL tools. PPO (RL.5) improves a policy with an advantage, "how much better than expected was this?", and clips each update so the policy can't move too far at once. GRPO (M3.5) keeps that clipped update but drops PPO's value network: it samples a group of answers to the same prompt and uses the group's mean reward as the baseline.

RLVR combines that with a reward a program can compute: does the final answer match the key, do the hidden unit tests pass, is the output in the required format. For reasoning models, the prompts are maths, code, logic puzzles and other problems with checkable answers, and each response is a long chain of thought followed by an answer.

Compared with STaR, three things change:

  • Failures teach too. Below-average responses get a negative advantage, so their habits (skipping a check, guessing) become less likely.
  • On-policy. Every step samples from the current model, so it learns from the mistakes it makes now, not the ones it made an iteration ago.
  • Nobody writes the reasoning. Only the final answer is checked. The model is free to discover how long to think, when to re-check and when to try another approach, as long as it ends up right more often.

The catch: RLVR only works where answers can be checked automatically, and only learns from prompts the model sometimes solves. If all $G$ responses get the same reward, every advantage is 0 and the prompt teaches nothing (M3.5). That's why recipes pick or filter prompts by difficulty.

Explain like I'm 5

You have a big pile of maths practice sheets, and the answers are printed at the back. Nobody watches you work. You try each problem a few different ways, then check the back.

Every time a way of working gets the right answer, you do more of that; every time one gets it wrong, you do less. After weeks of this you notice something: you've started slowing down and double-checking before you peek, because the times you double-checked were the times you got it right.

Where it breaks: the model doesn't "notice" anything. RL just makes the token patterns that came before right answers more likely. And if the answer key could be fooled, say by writing two answers, the model would learn to do that instead (RS.4).

Which of these does RLVR with GRPO do that STaR's filter-and-fine-tune loop doesn't?

  1. Use the correctness of the final answer as the signal
  2. Generate the training rationales with the model itself
  3. Need human-written chains of thought for every question
  4. Push down below-average responses, using fresh samples from the current model
Both use final-answer correctness and self-generated reasoning. RLVR adds negative advantages for worse-than-average attempts and stays on-policy. Neither needs human-written chains.

o1 and DeepSeek-R1

OpenAI o1 (September 2024, "Learning to reason with LLMs") was the first widely known reasoning model: it spends a long, hidden chain of thought before answering. OpenAI disclosed few training details. The announcement says a large-scale reinforcement-learning algorithm teaches the model to use its chain of thought productively, and that performance keeps improving both with more RL training compute and with more thinking time at inference. Users see a summary, not the raw chain.

DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, January 2025; later published in Nature) described a recipe in the open, with open weights.

R1-Zero started from the base model DeepSeek-V3-Base and applied GRPO directly, with no supervised fine-tuning first. The reward was purely rule-based: an accuracy reward (correct final answer; tests for code) and a format reward (reasoning inside <think> tags). The paper reports that:

  • Accuracy rose steadily: pass@1 on the AIME 2024 maths competition went from 15.6% to 71.0% over training.
  • The average response length kept growing: the model learned by itself to spend more tokens on harder problems.
  • Behaviours nobody programmed appeared: re-checking work, trying alternative approaches, and an "aha moment" where it stops mid-solution to re-evaluate ("Wait, …").
  • But the chains were often hard to read and mixed languages.

R1 fixed the readability with a multi-stage pipeline:

  1. Cold start: SFT on a few thousand long, readable chain-of-thought examples.
  2. Reasoning RL as in R1-Zero, plus a language-consistency reward (share of the chain in the target language).
  3. Rejection sampling + SFT: sample from the RL model, keep good responses (about 600k reasoning samples) plus about 200k non-reasoning ones (writing, factual QA, translation), and fine-tune on them.
  4. RL for all scenarios: rule-based rewards for reasoning, reward models for helpfulness and harmlessness.

Distillation. DeepSeek also fine-tuned smaller open models (Qwen2.5 and Llama, 1.5B to 70B parameters) with plain SFT on about 800k samples generated by R1, no RL at all. These distilled models reasoned far better than their bases, and better than running large-scale RL directly on a 32B base model in the authors' comparison. Their conclusion: for small models, learning from a strong reasoner's traces is cheaper and more effective than discovering reasoning through RL alone. Distillation (M5.2) has since become a standard way to make small reasoning models.

Why did DeepSeek-R1 add a "cold start" SFT stage that R1-Zero didn't have?

  1. R1-Zero's pure-RL chains were often hard to read and mixed languages; a small set of readable examples fixed the style before RL
  2. RL can't work at all without SFT first
  3. To replace the rule-based rewards with human ratings
  4. To make the model's answers shorter
R1-Zero showed that pure RL from a base model works, so SFT isn't required. But readability and language mixing were problems, which the cold-start data (and a language-consistency reward) addressed.

Thinking budgets: more isn't always better

A reasoning model's answer quality depends on how long it thinks, so as of 2026 many APIs and open models expose a dial: a reasoning-effort setting (low, medium, high), a maximum number of thinking tokens, or a switch between thinking and non-thinking modes in one model. Muennighoff et al. (2025, "s1") showed a blunt version called budget forcing: to cut thinking short, force the end-of-thinking marker; to extend it, suppress the marker and append "Wait", which often makes the model re-check and fix its answer.

More thinking isn't always better. Reasoning models can overthink, spending thousands of tokens on "what is 2 + 3?" (Chen et al., 2024). And whether RL creates genuinely new reasoning ability or mainly makes the model more reliable at finding solutions it could already sample is debated: Yue et al. (2025) found that base models catch up with their RL-trained versions on pass@k at large $k$, while ProRL (Liu et al., 2025) reports that longer, broader RL training can expand what the model solves.

What does "budget forcing" (Muennighoff et al., 2025) do to extend a model's thinking?

  1. Retrains the model with a longer context window
  2. Raises the temperature so the model writes more
  3. Suppresses the end-of-thinking marker and appends "Wait", so the model keeps reasoning
  4. Samples 16 answers and votes
It's a pure decoding-time trick. Forcing the end marker cuts thinking short; appending "Wait" extends it, which often triggers a re-check.

What thinking costs

Every thinking token is a full forward pass (RS.1), generated one after another. So a reasoning model's strengths come with a bill, both when you serve it and when you train it.

Worked example: what an answer costs

Take an illustrative price of $\$10$ per million output tokens (thinking tokens are typically billed as output, even when hidden) and a decoding speed of 60 tokens per second.

  1. Non-reasoning answer, 300 tokens: $300 \times 10 / 10^6 = \$0.003$, and $300 / 60 = 5$ seconds.
  2. Reasoning answer, 4,000 thinking + 300 answer tokens: $4300 \times 10 / 10^6 = \$0.043$, and $4300 / 60 \approx 72$ seconds.
  3. Ratio: about 14× the cost and the wait, for the same visible answer. Worth it for a hard proof; wasteful for "what time zone is Lisbon in?"

Why this matters in practice: as of 2026, an RLVR stage with GRPO or a close variant is a common part of post-training for reasoning and coding models, alongside SFT and preference tuning, though recipes vary a lot and the largest labs publish few details. If you deploy a reasoning model, the thinking budget is a real cost and latency knob: route easy requests to a low effort setting (or a non-reasoning model) and save long thinking for hard ones. If you train small models, distilling traces from a strong reasoner is usually the first thing to try.

A reasoning model thinks for 2,700 tokens, then writes a 300-token answer. A non-reasoning model writes the same 300-token answer directly. At the same price per output token, how much more does the reasoning answer cost?

  1. The same, because hidden thinking tokens are free
  2. About 10×
  3. About 9×
  4. About 2,700×
$(2700 + 300) / 300 = 10$. Thinking tokens are generated like any others and are typically billed as output, even when you never see them.
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

How did DeepSeek-R1-Zero learn to reason without anyone writing reasoning examples for it?

Model answer: Starting from a base model, it sampled groups of long responses to problems with checkable answers, scored each with rule-based accuracy and format rewards, and updated with GRPO so that responses better than their group's average became more likely. Because only the final answer was checked, any habit that tended to precede correct answers, such as thinking longer or re-checking, was reinforced, so longer and self-verifying chains emerged on their own.
Check yourself

In GRPO, what replaces PPO's value network as the baseline?

  1. A frozen copy of the reference model
  2. The mean (and std) of the rewards of several responses sampled for the same prompt
  3. A process reward model's step scores
  4. The reward of the previous training step
The group is the baseline: $\hat A_i = (r_i - \text{mean})/\text{std}$ (M3.5). The reference model is still there, but only for the KL penalty.

What did DeepSeek's "distilled" R1 models involve?

  1. Running RL with GRPO on each small model from scratch
  2. Pruning R1's weights down to 1.5B–70B parameters
  3. Matching R1's logits token by token during pretraining
  4. Supervised fine-tuning of smaller Qwen and Llama models on about 800k samples generated by R1
Plain SFT on R1's outputs, no RL. In the authors' comparison, this beat doing large-scale RL directly on a 32B base model.

What did OpenAI publicly say about how o1 was trained?

  1. Little: large-scale RL teaches it to use its chain of thought, and performance improves with more RL compute and more thinking time
  2. The full recipe, including reward functions and data
  3. That it was trained only with SFT on human-written proofs
  4. That it uses majority voting over 64 samples for every answer
OpenAI disclosed few details. The public claims were about RL on chains of thought and scaling with both train-time and test-time compute.

An RLVR run's training reward jumps, but samples show many responses containing two different boxed answers. Which earlier idea explains this?

  1. The KV cache is too small
  2. Self-consistency voting is too strong
  3. Reward hacking: the checker accepts any matching answer, so listing several raises the reward (M3.5)
  4. The learning rate is too low
The policy optimizes the checker you wrote. Strict parsing (exactly one final answer) closes this hole; RS.4 looks at why such holes matter even more for learned verifiers.

Key takeaways

  • STaR (2022): generate rationales, keep the ones with correct answers, fine-tune, repeat; it learns only from successes.
  • RLVR: on-policy RL (GRPO/PPO-style) with rule-based rewards on checkable tasks; failures get pushed down too, and nobody writes the reasoning.
  • o1 (2024) popularised reasoning models with few disclosed details; R1-Zero (2025) showed pure RL from a base model grows longer, self-checking chains; R1 added cold-start SFT and more stages for readability.
  • Distillation of reasoning traces is the cheap way to strong small reasoners.
  • Thinking costs tokens, money and time, at inference and in training; thinking budgets control it.
