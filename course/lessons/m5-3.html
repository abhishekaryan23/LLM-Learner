Mixture of experts

Lesson 3 of Optimization

What if a model could hold 47 billion parameters but use only about 13 billion of them for each token? That's a mixture of experts: many specialist sub-networks, plus a router that picks a couple of them per token. Most of today's largest open models are built this way.

  • Explain why MoE training needs load balancing, and what expert capacity and dropped tokens are.
  • Say why MoE buys more capacity per FLOP but costs more memory and communication.

From one MLP to many experts

Each transformer block (M1.4) has attention followed by an MLP, and in a typical dense LLM the MLPs hold around two-thirds of all the parameters. A mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer replaces that one MLP with $E$ separate MLPs, the experts, and adds a small router (or gating network) that chooses which experts each token uses:

$$p = \mathrm{softmax}(W_r\,x),\qquad y = \sum_{i \in \mathrm{TopK}(p)} g_i\,E_i(x),\qquad g_i = \frac{p_i}{\sum_{j \in \mathrm{TopK}(p)} p_j}$$
  • $x$ is one token's hidden vector, and $W_r$ is the router's weight matrix, one row per expert. So $p$ holds one probability per expert.
  • $\mathrm{TopK}(p)$ is the set of the $k$ experts with the highest probability, typically $k = 1$ or $2$ (some recent models use 8 of 256 small experts).
  • $E_i(x)$ is expert $i$'s output, an ordinary MLP applied to $x$.
  • $g_i$ is the gate: the chosen experts' probabilities rescaled to sum to 1. (Some designs, like the Switch Transformer, use $p_i$ directly.)

Every token can use different experts, in every layer. The idea goes back to Shazeer et al. (2017, “Outrageously Large Neural Networks”); GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2021) brought it to transformers, and the Switch Transformer (Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, 2022) simplified it to $k = 1$ and scaled it to 1.6 trillion parameters.

Explain like I'm 5

A hospital has a robot receptionist and a whole corridor of specialist doctors. Each patient who walks in is sent to just one or two doctors who suit them best. The other doctors rest until a patient who needs them arrives.

So the hospital knows a huge amount (lots of doctors), but each visit is quick (only a couple of doctors per patient). If the receptionist sends everyone to the same doctor, a long queue forms, so it has to spread patients out.

Where the analogy breaks: real experts don't specialize in neat topics like “hearts” or “bones”. They tend to pick up patterns in tokens and syntax that no one designed, and every layer has its own receptionist.

Total versus active parameters

MoE splits “model size” into two numbers. Total parameters is everything stored, which sets the memory bill. Active parameters is what one token actually uses, which sets the compute: a forward pass costs about $2 \times$ (active parameters) FLOPs per token.

Worked example: a Mixtral-like model

Mixtral 8x7B (Jiang et al., 2024) has 32 layers, hidden size 4096, and 8 experts per layer with top-2 routing. Each expert is a SwiGLU MLP with three weight matrices of size $4096 \times 14336$.

  1. One expert: $3 \times 4096 \times 14336 \approx 176.2$M parameters.
  2. All experts: $32 \text{ layers} \times 8 \times 176.2\text{M} \approx 45.1$B.
  3. Shared parts: attention ($\approx 41.9$M per layer with grouped-query attention, so $\approx 1.34$B) plus input and output embeddings ($2 \times 32000 \times 4096 \approx 0.26$B), about 1.6B in all.
  4. Total: $45.1 + 1.6 \approx 46.7$B. Active: $32 \times 2 \times 176.2\text{M} \approx 11.3$B in experts, plus the 1.6B shared $\approx 12.9$B.

That matches Mixtral's reported figures of roughly 47B total and 13B active. Note it's not $8 \times 7 = 56$B: only the MLPs are copied 8 times, while attention and embeddings are shared. Each token costs about $2 \times 12.9 \approx 26$ GFLOPs, like a 13B dense model, but the weights need about 93 GB in bf16, like a 47B one.

Keeping the experts busy: load balancing

Left alone, routers collapse. A few experts get slightly better early on, so they get more tokens, so they get better still, while the rest starve and stop learning. That wastes parameters, and it also overloads the GPUs holding the popular experts.

The Switch Transformer adds an auxiliary load-balancing loss to the training loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha \cdot E \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i\,P_i$$
  • $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens in the batch actually routed to expert $i$.
  • $P_i$ is the router's average probability for expert $i$ over the same tokens.
  • $\alpha$ is a small weight (0.01 in the Switch paper), and $E$ the number of experts.

With perfectly even routing, every $f_i = P_i = 1/E$, and the sum times $E$ equals 1, its minimum. Why both terms? The counts $f_i$ come from a hard top-k choice and have no gradient, while $P_i$ is a smooth softmax output, so the gradient flows through $P_i$ and pushes probability away from the over-used experts.

Balancing has a hardware side too. Each expert gets a fixed capacity, a buffer for $C = \text{capacity factor} \times \text{tokens} / E$ tokens per batch. Tokens beyond it are dropped: they skip that expert layer and pass through on the residual connection. A capacity factor a little above 1 (such as 1.25) absorbs random fluctuation at the cost of some padding. Newer systems refine all this: a router z-loss keeps router logits small for stability (Zoph et al., 2022), “dropless” implementations avoid dropping tokens at all, and DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI, 2024) balances with no auxiliary loss, by adding a per-expert bias to the routing scores and nudging it after each step.

Expert parallelism and the memory bill

An MoE model's experts usually don't fit on one GPU, so they're spread out: GPU 1 holds some experts, GPU 2 others, and so on. This is expert parallelism. In every MoE layer, each GPU sends each token to whichever GPUs hold its chosen experts (an all-to-all exchange), the experts run, and a second all-to-all sends the results back. That communication is why MoE training and serving lean heavily on fast interconnects, and it combines with the data and tensor parallelism from M2.5.

The trade-off, in one line: MoE gives more capacity per FLOP but needs more memory.

  • Capacity per FLOP: a model's knowledge grows with its total parameters, but the cost per token grows only with the active ones. The Switch Transformer reported up to 7× faster pretraining than a dense T5-Base using the same compute per token.
  • Memory: all experts must stay loaded. Mixtral computes like a 13B model but needs the memory of a 47B one, which is one reason quantization (M5.1) is so popular for MoE models.
  • Bandwidth: for a single stream, each token reads only its active experts' weights, which makes MoE decoding fast. In a large batch, different tokens hit different experts, so nearly all expert weights get read every step, and the advantage shifts to compute (more on batching in M5.6).
  • Practicalities: routing adds communication, uneven loads cause stragglers, and MoE models have a reputation for being trickier to train and fine-tune stably.
Why this matters in practice: many of the largest open-weight models of 2025–2026 are MoE. DeepSeek-V3 has 671B total and 37B active parameters (256 small routed experts, 8 per token, plus one shared expert that every token uses). Qwen3-235B-A22B puts the counts in its name: 235B total, 22B active. OpenAI's gpt-oss-120b has about 117B total and 5.1B active. When you read a model card, look for both numbers: total tells you the hardware you need, active tells you roughly how fast it runs.
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Why can Mixtral 8x7B compute each token about as cheaply as a 13B dense model, yet need the GPU memory of a 47B model?

Model answer: In every layer its router sends each token to only 2 of 8 expert MLPs, so a token touches about 13B parameters (the 2 experts plus the shared attention and embeddings) and the FLOPs scale with that. But different tokens choose different experts, so all 8 experts in every layer, about 47B parameters, must be loaded and ready.
Check yourself

A model has 3B shared parameters and 32 MoE layers, each with 64 experts of 20M parameters, routing to the top 4. How many parameters are active per token?

  1. About 5.6B
  2. About 44B
  3. About 2.6B
  4. About 3.1B
Active $= 3\text{B} + 32 \times 4 \times 0.02\text{B} = 3 + 2.56 \approx 5.6$B. About 44B is the total ($3 + 32 \times 64 \times 0.02$), and 2.56B forgets the shared parameters.

In the Switch load-balancing loss $\alpha E \sum_i f_i P_i$, why include the mean router probability $P_i$ and not just the token fractions $f_i$?

  1. $P_i$ makes the loss larger, so it trains faster
  2. The fractions $f_i$ always equal $1/E$
  3. $f_i$ comes from a hard top-k choice and has no gradient; $P_i$ is differentiable, so the router can learn from it
  4. $P_i$ counts dropped tokens
Counting which experts won is a discrete operation. The product $f_i P_i$ is large when an expert is both popular and highly scored, and the gradient through $P_i$ pushes the router away from it.

In a Switch-style MoE with a fixed capacity, what happens to a token routed to an expert that is already full?

  1. Training stops with an error
  2. It's dropped from that layer's expert computation and passes through on the residual connection
  3. It waits for the next batch
  4. It's sent to every other expert instead
Overflow tokens skip the expert, so their output for that layer is just the residual stream. A capacity factor above 1, or good balancing, keeps this rare; dropless designs avoid it entirely.

Why is 4-bit weight quantization (M5.1) especially attractive for serving an MoE model?

  1. It removes the need for a router
  2. Quantization only works on experts
  3. It reduces the number of active parameters
  4. Memory is MoE's main cost, since all experts must be resident; quantization cuts that footprint roughly 4× versus bf16
MoE already saves compute through sparse activation; what it doesn't save is memory. Quantizing the experts attacks exactly that. The active parameter count doesn't change, only their bytes.

Key takeaways

  • An MoE layer swaps one MLP for $E$ expert MLPs plus a router; each token uses only its top-k experts, weighted by gates.
  • Total parameters set memory; active parameters set compute (about $2\times$ active FLOPs per token). Mixtral 8x7B: about 47B total, 13B active.
  • Routers collapse without load balancing (an auxiliary loss or a bias update); fixed capacity means overflow tokens get dropped.
  • Expert parallelism spreads experts across GPUs, with all-to-all communication in every MoE layer.
  • MoE buys more capacity per FLOP, paid for in memory and communication.
