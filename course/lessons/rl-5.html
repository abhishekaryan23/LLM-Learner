Actor-critic & PPO

Lesson 5 of Reinforcement learning

REINFORCE works, but slowly and noisily. Give the policy a partner that predicts how well things should go, and every step can be graded on the spot. Add a rule that stops any single update from going too far, and you have PPO, the workhorse behind RLHF.

A critic predicted a state was worth 0.60. After one move with reward 0, it predicts the new state is worth 0.80 (with $\gamma = 0.9$). Was the move better or worse than expected?

  1. Better: $0 + 0.9 \times 0.80 = 0.72$ beats the 0.60 expected
  2. Worse: the reward was 0
  3. Exactly as expected
  4. You can't tell until the episode ends
That difference, $0.72 - 0.60 = +0.12$, is the TD error. Actor-critic methods use it immediately as the advantage of the move.

After one gradient step, a token's probability went from 0.10 to 0.50. Why might that worry an RL engineer?

  1. Probabilities above 0.5 are not allowed
  2. It means the learning rate was too small
  3. It's fine; bigger steps always learn faster
  4. The batch was sampled from the old policy, and one noisy estimate has moved the policy a long way; it may have got much worse
A fivefold change from one batch is a huge jump. Trust-region methods such as TRPO and PPO exist to prevent exactly this.

Actor-critic: a learned baseline

REINFORCE (RL.4) has two weaknesses: it must wait for the episode to end to know $G_t$, and $G_t$ is noisy. A baseline helps, and the best baseline is the state's value $V(s)$. So why not learn $V$ alongside the policy?

That is the actor-critic architecture, introduced by Andrew Barto, Richard Sutton and Charles Anderson in 1983 for balancing a pole on a cart. The actor is the policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$. The critic is a value function $V_\phi(s)$, trained by TD learning (RL.3). The critic's job is to tell the actor, at every step, whether things went better or worse than expected: the advantage

$$A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)$$

Positive means "this action beat this state's average"; negative means it did worse. We don't have $Q$, but the one-step TD error estimates it:

$$\delta_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)$$

If the critic were exact, the average of $\delta_t$ over what can happen next would be exactly $Q^\pi(s_t,a_t) - V^\pi(s_t)$, the true advantage. So the actor follows $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,\delta_t$, and the critic regresses $V_\phi(s_t)$ toward $r_{t+1} + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})$. In our gridworld:

Modern deep actor-critics such as A2C/A3C (Mnih et al., 2016) run many environments in parallel to collect experience fast. The price of using the critic is bias: early on, $V_\phi$ is wrong, and so is every $\delta$ computed from it.

$V(s) = 1.0$, the agent gets $r = 0.5$ and lands in $s'$ with $V(s') = 0.4$; $\gamma = 0.9$. What does the actor-critic do with that action?

  1. Makes it more likely, because the reward was positive
  2. Nothing, because the TD error is zero
  3. Makes it less likely: $\delta = 0.5 + 0.36 - 1.0 = -0.14$
  4. Makes it more likely: $\delta = +0.86$
$\delta = 0.5 + 0.9 \times 0.4 - 1.0 = -0.14$. A positive reward that leads somewhere worse than expected is a below-average move.

GAE: dialling bias against variance

The one-step $\delta_t$ has low variance (one random step) but inherits the critic's errors. The full Monte Carlo advantage $G_t - V(s_t)$ is unbiased but noisy. In between are $n$-step estimates that use $n$ real rewards before trusting the critic. Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE; Schulman, Moritz, Levine, Jordan & Abbeel, 2015) averages all of them with exponentially decaying weights, which works out to a simple sum of future TD errors:

$$\hat A_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l} = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\delta_{t+1} + (\gamma\lambda)^2\,\delta_{t+2} + \dots$$
  • $\lambda = 0$: just $\delta_t$. Lowest variance, most bias.
  • $\lambda = 1$: the sum telescopes to $G_t - V(s_t)$. No bias from the critic, highest variance.
  • In between (0.95 is a common default), the weights fade over roughly $1/(1-\gamma\lambda)$ steps.

Worked example

Three TD errors along an episode: $\delta_t = 0.12$, $\delta_{t+1} = -0.05$, $\delta_{t+2} = 0.30$, then the episode ends. $\gamma = 0.9$, $\lambda = 0.95$, so $\gamma\lambda = 0.855$.

  1. Weights: $1,\ 0.855,\ 0.855^2 = 0.731$.
  2. $\hat A_t = 0.12 + 0.855 \times (-0.05) + 0.731 \times 0.30 = 0.12 - 0.043 + 0.219 = 0.297$.
  3. The good news two steps later raises this step's advantage from 0.12 to about 0.30: credit flows back to earlier actions.

Explain like I'm 5

A kid is learning to skateboard with a coach watching. The kid is the actor: they try moves. The coach is the critic: before each move the coach has a feeling for how it will go.

After every move, the coach says "better than I expected!" or "worse than I expected". The kid does more of the "better" moves. The coach also updates their own expectations, so the feedback gets sharper over time.

Where it breaks: a real coach knows skateboarding already. The critic starts out knowing nothing and learns at the same time as the actor, so early feedback can be plain wrong.

You set GAE's $\lambda = 1$. What advantage estimate do you get?

  1. Just the one-step TD error $\delta_t$
  2. The Monte Carlo return minus the value, $G_t - V(s_t)$: unbiased but high-variance
  3. Zero
  4. The critic's value $V(s_t)$ itself
With $\lambda = 1$ the weighted TD errors telescope into the full discounted return minus $V(s_t)$. That's REINFORCE with a value baseline.

Trust regions: from TRPO to PPO

Policy gradients have a nasty failure mode: one overly large step can wreck the policy, and since the policy also collects the next batch of data, a bad policy produces bad data and may never recover. The gradient only says which way is uphill locally.

TRPO (Trust Region Policy Optimization; Schulman et al., 2015) maximizes the expected advantage under a hard limit on how far the new policy may move, measured by the KL divergence (P1.6) between old and new action distributions. It works well but needs second-order machinery (conjugate gradients on the Fisher matrix). PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization; Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford & Klimov, 2017) gets most of the benefit with a first-order trick. Write the probability ratio between the new policy and the one that collected the data,

$$\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}$$

and maximize the clipped surrogate objective:

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t\,\hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big]$$

$\hat A_t$ is the (GAE) advantage and $\epsilon$, typically 0.2, is the half-width of the allowed band. Once a good token's probability has risen more than 20% (or a bad token's has fallen more than 20%), the objective goes flat and that token stops pushing. The min means the clip only removes incentives to move further; moves in the wrong direction keep their full penalty. Because each update stays near $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, PPO can safely run several epochs of minibatch updates on every batch of samples.

Worked example ($\epsilon = 0.2$)

  1. $\hat A = +0.5$, $\rho = 1.4$: $\rho\hat A = 0.70$; clipped $1.2 \times 0.5 = 0.60$; min $= 0.60$. Flat: no further push up.
  2. $\hat A = -1$, $\rho = 0.7$: $\rho\hat A = -0.70$; clipped $0.8 \times (-1) = -0.80$; min $= -0.80$. Flat: no further push down.
  3. $\hat A = -1$, $\rho = 1.3$: $\rho\hat A = -1.30$; clipped $-1.20$; min $= -1.30$. Not clipped: the bad token got more likely, and the full gradient pulls it back.

Why can PPO reuse each batch of samples for several epochs, while vanilla policy gradient usually takes one step per batch?

  1. PPO uses a bigger learning rate
  2. PPO doesn't use advantages
  3. PPO's samples come from a fixed dataset of human answers
  4. The ratio corrects for the policy having moved, and the clip stops it from moving far from the data-collecting policy
$\rho_t$ re-weights old samples for the new policy, which is only reliable nearby; the clip enforces "nearby". Without it, repeated steps on the same batch overfit and can collapse the policy.

PPO for language models, and GRPO

RLHF (M3.3) runs exactly this machinery with tokens as actions. The actor is the LLM. The critic is a value model, usually a copy of a similar-sized LLM with a scalar head, predicting $V$ at every token position. A response gets a single reward at the end, from a reward model; GAE spreads it back over the tokens (RLHF setups commonly use $\gamma = 1$, so only $\lambda$ does the fading). Each token gets its own ratio $\rho_t$ and clipped term.

One addition is specific to RLHF: a KL penalty to a frozen reference model, $R = r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref})$, folded into the reward. PPO's clip limits how far each update moves from the previous policy; the KL term limits how far the whole run drifts from the SFT model, which keeps the policy where the reward model's judgement still holds (M3.3 covers it with a worked example).

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization; Shao et al., 2024, in the DeepSeekMath paper) removes the critic entirely. For each prompt it samples a group of $G$ responses, scores them, and uses the group's statistics as the baseline: $\hat A_i = (r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r)) / \operatorname{std}(r)$, shared by every token of response $i$. The rest is PPO's clipped objective plus a KL term in the loss. No value network means roughly half the trained parameters and nothing to fit; in exchange you generate several responses per prompt. With verifier rewards it became the standard recipe for reasoning models after DeepSeek-R1 (2025); see M3.5 and RS.3.

Why this matters for LLMs: the family tree is short. REINFORCE gives the direction (raise log-probs of good samples). A baseline, learned (PPO's critic) or computed from a group (GRPO, RLOO), turns rewards into advantages. A trust region (clip and/or KL) keeps each step safe. As of 2026 most RL post-training of frontier models is some variant of this recipe; when a run misbehaves, the usual suspects are the advantage estimate, the clip range and the KL coefficient.

What does GRPO use instead of a learned critic?

  1. The mean (and std) of rewards across several responses sampled for the same prompt
  2. The reward model's hidden states
  3. A Q-table over tokens
  4. The reference model's log-probabilities
The group is the baseline: $\hat A_i = (r_i - \text{mean})/\text{std}$. It's a Monte Carlo estimate of how good this prompt usually goes, with no value network to train.
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Explain what PPO's clip does and why the objective takes a minimum.

Model answer: The ratio $\rho = \pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}$ measures how much an update has changed a token's probability. Clipping it to $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ makes the objective flat (zero gradient) once a good token has become more than 20% more likely, or a bad one more than 20% less likely, so no single batch can move the policy far. Taking the min with the unclipped term keeps the full gradient when the policy has moved the wrong way, so the clip never hides a mistake.

$\epsilon = 0.2$, $\hat A = +2$, $\rho = 0.9$. What is PPO's clipped objective for this token?

  1. 2.4
  2. 1.6
  3. 1.8
  4. 0
$\rho$ is inside $[0.8, 1.2]$, so both terms equal $0.9 \times 2 = 1.8$. Clipping only bites outside the band.

In PPO-based RLHF, what is the difference between the clip and the KL penalty?

  1. They are the same thing written two ways
  2. The clip limits drift from the SFT model; the KL limits each update
  3. The KL penalty is only used in GRPO
  4. The clip limits each update relative to the policy that sampled the batch; the KL penalty limits drift from the frozen reference (SFT) model over the whole run
Two different leashes: one per step (clip, relative to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$), one overall (KL, relative to $\pi_\text{ref}$). See M3.3.

Connect to RL.2: the TD error $\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$ is zero on average when…

  1. $V$ is the true value of the policy, so it satisfies the Bellman expectation equation
  2. the reward is always zero
  3. $\gamma = 0$
  4. the policy is deterministic
The Bellman expectation equation says $V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}[r + \gamma V^\pi(s')]$. Under the policy's own actions, $\delta$ averages to zero; individual actions above or below that average have positive or negative advantage.

Key takeaways

  • Actor-critic: the actor (policy) learns from advantages; the critic $V_\phi$ learns by TD. The TD error $\delta$ is a one-step advantage estimate.
  • GAE: $\hat A_t = \sum_l (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$. $\lambda$ trades the critic's bias ($\lambda = 0$) against Monte Carlo variance ($\lambda = 1$).
  • TRPO bounds each update's KL; PPO clips the ratio $\rho$ to $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ with a min, so it's simple and can reuse batches.
  • RLHF = PPO on tokens + a value model + a KL penalty to the reference. GRPO replaces the critic with group-relative advantages.
