Reward models

Lesson 2 of Post-training

People can't give every answer a reliable “goodness score”, but they can usually tell which of two answers is better. A reward model turns thousands of those comparisons into a function that scores any response with a single number.

Why do most RLHF pipelines ask people to compare two answers instead of rating each one from 1 to 10?

  1. Comparisons need fewer answers to be generated
  2. A 1–10 rating can't be turned into a training signal
  3. Comparisons are quicker and more consistent: people disagree a lot about what a “7” means
  4. It's a legal requirement for human data
Absolute scales drift between people and even within one person over a day. “Which is better?” is an easier, more reliable question.

A reward model scores answer A at 3.0 and answer B at 1.0. Now add 10 to every score. What happens to the predicted probability that A beats B?

  1. Nothing. It only depends on the difference, which is still 2.0.
  2. It rises, because both scores are higher
  3. It falls toward 0.5
  4. It becomes exactly 1
The Bradley–Terry model uses only $r_A - r_B$. So a reward's absolute value means nothing on its own, which is why rewards are often normalized.

Why comparisons?

SFT (M3.1) needs someone to write the ideal answer, which is slow and caps the model at the writer's level. Asking for an absolute score is fast but noisy. Asking “which of these two is better?” is fast and fairly consistent. People are much better judges than authors.

A typical collection loop looks like this:

  1. Take a prompt $x$ and sample several responses, usually from the SFT model.
  2. A labeler compares them. InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) had labelers rank 4 to 9 responses per prompt, which yields up to $\binom{K}{2}$ pairs from one ranking.
  3. Each comparison becomes a triple $(x,\ y_w,\ y_l)$: the prompt, the chosen (winning) response and the rejected (losing) one.

The labels are noisy: in InstructGPT, labelers agreed with each other only about 73% of the time. Preference data captures a tendency, not ground truth, and that is exactly what the probabilistic model below is built for.

The reward model

A reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ is usually a copy of the SFT model (or another pretrained LM) with its output layer swapped out. Instead of projecting the final hidden state onto the vocabulary, a small linear scalar head maps the hidden state at the last token to one number. Same transformer, one new output; $\phi$ is all of its parameters.

InstructGPT used a 6B-parameter reward model alongside a 175B policy; they reported that a 175B reward model trained less stably. Today the reward model is often about as large as the policy.

Bradley–Terry: from scores to probabilities

How do we train a score from comparisons? The Bradley–Terry model (Bradley & Terry, 1952) says the probability that $y_w$ beats $y_l$ depends only on the difference of their scores:

$$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

$\succ$ means “is preferred to”, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid, which squashes any number into $(0, 1)$. Equal rewards give 0.5; a gap of $+2$ gives 0.88. (The same model drives Elo ratings and chatbot leaderboards, M4.5.)

Training maximizes the probability of the choices people actually made, which is the usual negative log-likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}(\phi) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,\,y_w,\,y_l)}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big)\Big]$$

$\mathbb{E}$ is the average over the preference dataset. This is just binary cross-entropy where the label is always “the chosen one wins”. Writing $\Delta = r_w - r_l$, the gradients are

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial r_w} = -\big(1 - \sigma(\Delta)\big), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial r_l} = +\big(1 - \sigma(\Delta)\big)$$

Gradient descent therefore pushes the chosen reward up and the rejected reward down, hardest when the model currently gets the pair wrong ($\sigma(\Delta)$ small) and barely at all once it is confidently right.

Explain like I'm 5

At a talent show, the judge doesn't give marks out of 100. Two kids perform, and the judge just raises a star over the better one.

A robot watches thousands of these star moments. Slowly it learns to give every act its own secret score, so that whoever has the higher score is usually the one who got the star.

Now the robot can score acts the judge never saw.

Where it breaks: the robot never learns what good singing is, only what tended to get stars. If loud acts often won, it may love anything loud. That's reward hacking.

Worked example

The reward model scores the chosen response $r_w = 2.0$ and the rejected one $r_l = 0.5$.

  1. Difference: $\Delta = 2.0 - 0.5 = 1.5$.
  2. Bradley–Terry probability: $\sigma(1.5) = 1/(1 + e^{-1.5}) = 1/1.223 = 0.82$.
  3. Loss: $-\log 0.82 = 0.20$.
  4. Gradient on the chosen reward: $-(1 - 0.82) = -0.18$, so gradient descent nudges $r_w$ up, and $r_l$ down by the same amount.

Reward hacking and overoptimization

The reward model is only a proxy for what people want, learned from a finite, noisy dataset. In the next lesson, a policy is trained to push this score as high as it can. Push hard enough and it finds responses the reward model rates highly for the wrong reasons. This is Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Gao et al., 2022 (“Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization”) measured this cleanly. They used a large “gold” reward model as a stand-in for people, trained smaller proxy reward models on its labels, then optimized policies against the proxies. The proxy score kept climbing, while the gold score rose, peaked and then fell. Larger proxy reward models and more data delayed the fall, but didn't remove it.

Curves are illustrative shapes, not data. Gao et al. fit the gold score as a function of $\sqrt{\text{KL}}$ between the optimized policy and its starting point.

Hacks seen in practice include length (longer answers tend to score higher; Singhal et al., 2023), sycophancy (agreeing with the user), confident tone, and heavy formatting. Common defences:

  • A KL penalty that keeps the policy close to the SFT model (M3.3).
  • Early stopping, judged by held-out human or stronger-model evaluations rather than by the reward itself.
  • Bigger reward models, more and fresher preference data, including comparisons of the current policy's own outputs.
  • Ensembles of reward models, and explicit length controls.
Why this matters in practice: reward models are used well beyond PPO. They drive best-of-n and rejection sampling (generate several answers, keep the top-scored one; Llama 2 and 3 used this), filter training data, and serve as automatic judges, with benchmarks such as RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024) comparing them. DPO (M3.4) skips the explicit reward model but optimizes the very same Bradley–Terry objective. Where an answer can be checked automatically (maths, code), a verifier replaces the learned reward altogether (M3.5).
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

Nobody ever tells the reward model the “right” score for an answer. So how does it learn one?

Model answer: It is trained on pairs where a person preferred one response over another. The Bradley–Terry model turns the score difference into a probability, $\sigma(r_w - r_l)$, and training maximizes the probability of the observed choices, pushing chosen scores up and rejected ones down. Only differences are pinned down, so the scores mean “better or worse than”, not anything absolute.
Check yourself

A reward model gives the chosen and rejected responses exactly the same reward. What is the loss on this pair?

  1. 0, because the rewards are equal
  2. 1
  3. $\log 2 \approx 0.69$
  4. 0.5
$\Delta = 0$, so $P = \sigma(0) = 0.5$ and the loss is $-\log 0.5 = \log 2 \approx 0.69$. A coin-flip guess still costs something.

Connect to softmax (P3.2): the Bradley–Terry probability $\sigma(r_A - r_B)$ equals…

  1. $r_A / (r_A + r_B)$
  2. $e^{r_A} / (e^{r_A} + e^{r_B})$, a two-way softmax over the rewards
  3. $\mathrm{softmax}$ over the vocabulary at the last token
  4. $e^{r_A - r_B}$
Divide top and bottom by $e^{r_A}$: $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r_A - r_B)}} = \sigma(r_A - r_B)$. Option 1 breaks with negative rewards; option 4 isn't a probability.

During RL, the reward model's score keeps rising, but human ratings of the outputs start to drop. What's the most likely explanation?

  1. The learning rate is too small
  2. The human raters got tired
  3. The reward model is too accurate
  4. The policy is exploiting the reward model's errors (overoptimization), drifting too far from where it started
That's the Gao et al. picture: proxy up, true quality down. Fixes are a stronger KL penalty, earlier stopping, or a better reward model.

What does a typical LLM reward model output for a (prompt, response) pair?

  1. A single scalar, from a linear head on the last token's hidden state
  2. A probability distribution over the vocabulary
  3. The label “chosen” or “rejected”
  4. A human-written score for each token
The vocabulary projection is swapped for a scalar head. “Chosen/rejected” only exists in the training pairs; at use time the model outputs one number per response.

Key takeaways

  • Preference data is pairs $(x, y_w, y_l)$: comparing is easier and more consistent than scoring, though still noisy.
  • A reward model is an LM with a scalar head: (prompt, response) → one number.
  • Bradley–Terry: $P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma(r_w - r_l)$; loss $= -\log \sigma(r_w - r_l)$. Only differences matter.
  • Gradients push chosen up and rejected down, hardest when the model is wrong.
  • Optimize a proxy too hard and you get reward hacking; KL penalties, early stopping and better reward models limit it.
