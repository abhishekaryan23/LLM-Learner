Probability & expectation

Lesson 5 of Math you actually need

An LLM never simply "knows" the next word. At every step it produces a probability for every token in its vocabulary, then picks one. This lesson gives you the vocabulary for that: distributions, expectation, sampling, conditional probability, and the log trick that every training loss relies on.

By the end you can

  • Check that a discrete distribution is valid, and compute an expectation as a weighted average.
  • Explain how sampling from a distribution works, and what $P(\text{next token} \mid \text{context})$ means.
  • Explain why we multiply probabilities along a sequence but add log-probabilities in practice.

Probabilities over all the possible outcomes must add up to 1, so $1 - 0.5 - 0.3 = 0.2$.

Each of a sentence's 100 tokens got probability about 0.1 from the model. Roughly what probability does the model give the whole sentence?

  1. $10$ (add them up)
  2. $0.1$
  3. $10^{-100}$
  4. $1/100$
The probability of a sequence is the product of the per-token probabilities: $0.1^{100} = 10^{-100}$. Numbers that small are why we work with logs, as you'll see.

A distribution is a list of probabilities

A discrete probability distribution assigns a probability $p(x)$ to each possible outcome $x$ from a finite list. Two rules make it valid:

$$p(x) \ge 0 \text{ for every } x, \qquad \sum_{x} p(x) = 1$$

A fair die gives each face $\frac{1}{6}$. A language model's outcomes are the tokens in its vocabulary, typically tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand of them (Llama 2 has 32,000; Llama 3 has about 128,000). Expectation: the probability-weighted average

If each outcome $x$ carries a number (a payout, a length, a loss), its expected value is the average of those numbers, weighted by how likely each one is:

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{x} x \; p(x)$$

For a fair die: $\frac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = 3.5$. You can never roll a 3.5, so the expectation needn't be a possible outcome. It's what the long-run average settles towards: average many independent rolls and you'll get closer and closer to 3.5 (the law of large numbers).

This is more than a textbook idea. The training loss of a model is an expectation, "average loss over all the text the model could see", and the loss on a mini-batch is the sample average that estimates it (P2.2).

Worked example

A prize wheel pays 10 with probability 0.2, pays 2 with probability 0.5, and pays 0 with probability 0.3.

  1. Valid? $0.2 + 0.5 + 0.3 = 1$ ✓, and none is negative
  2. Weighted values: $10 \times 0.2 = 2$, $2 \times 0.5 = 1$, $0 \times 0.3 = 0$
  3. $\mathbb{E}[\text{payout}] = 2 + 1 + 0 = 3$ per spin, on average

Sampling: turning probabilities into a choice

To sample, lay the probabilities end to end along $[0, 1)$, draw a uniform random number $r$, and pick whichever slice it lands in. Bigger probability means a wider slice and more hits. In code:

r = random.random()          # uniform in [0, 1)
total = 0.0
for token, p in zip(tokens, probs):
    total += p
    if r < total:
        return token
return tokens[-1]            # guard against rounding

Text generation is this, repeated: compute a distribution, sample a token, append it, repeat. Always picking the single most likely token instead is greedy decoding. Settings like temperature and top-p reshape the distribution before sampling (M1.5).

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a bag of 100 marbles: 45 say "mat", 20 say "floor", 15 say "sofa", and so on. Close your eyes and pull one out. That's sampling: "mat" comes out most often, but not every time.

Now change the sentence to "The dog slept on the…". You get a different bag, with more "floor" marbles. That's what "conditional" means: which bag you use depends on the words before.

Where it breaks: a real model's bag has around 100,000 kinds of marble, the amounts don't have to be whole numbers, and the model builds a fresh bag for every single position.

Conditional probability: $P(A \mid B)$

$P(A \mid B)$ is read "the probability of $A$ given $B$": among the cases where $B$ happens, how often does $A$ happen too?

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}$$

Example: it's cloudy on 40% of days and rains on 24% of days, and it only rains when it's cloudy. Then $P(\text{rain} \mid \text{cloudy}) = 0.24 / 0.40 = 0.6$. Knowing it's cloudy raises the chance of rain from 24% to 60%.

A language model outputs exactly such a conditional distribution: $P(\text{next token} \mid \text{all the tokens so far})$. Chaining them gives the probability of a whole sequence, by the chain rule of probability:

$$P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n) = P(w_1)\, P(w_2 \mid w_1)\, P(w_3 \mid w_1, w_2) \cdots P(w_n \mid w_1, \dots, w_{n-1})$$

Each factor is one forward step of the model. "Autoregressive" just means "generates by using this factorisation, one token at a time".

Why we add logs instead of multiplying

That product is a problem in practice. Suppose a model gives "the" probability 0.05, then "cat" (given "the") 0.01, then "sat" (given "the cat") 0.2:

$$P = 0.05 \times 0.01 \times 0.2 = 0.0001 \qquad \ln P = (-2.996) + (-4.605) + (-1.609) = -9.210$$

Three tokens already give $10^{-4}$. A thousand tokens at about 0.1 each give $10^{-1000}$, far below the smallest positive 64-bit float (about $5 \times 10^{-324}$), so the computer rounds it to exactly 0. The log-probability, $1000 \times \ln 0.1 \approx -2302.6$, is a perfectly ordinary number. Logs fix three things at once:

  • No underflow: products of tiny numbers become sums of moderate negative numbers.
  • Same winner: $\ln$ is increasing, so whatever maximises the probability also maximises the log-probability.
  • Easier calculus: the derivative of a sum is the sum of derivatives (P1.3), one simple term per token.

In ML, "log" means the natural log $\ln$ unless stated otherwise. Log-probabilities are always $\le 0$, so we usually flip the sign and minimise the negative log-likelihood. Averaged per token, that's the LLM training loss, and it gets its full story in the next lesson.

Explain it back · in one or two sentences

Why do LLM training and evaluation work with log-probabilities instead of probabilities?

Model answer: A sequence's probability is a product of many per-token probabilities, which quickly underflows to 0 in floating point. Taking logs turns the product into a sum of manageable numbers, keeps the same best answer (log is increasing), and makes derivatives simple, one term per token.
Check yourself

Which of these is a valid probability distribution over three tokens?

  1. $(0.5, 0.3, 0.3)$
  2. $(0.7, -0.1, 0.4)$
  3. $(0.6, 0.4, 0)$
  4. $(0.2, 0.2, 0.2)$
It needs non-negative entries that sum to 1. A zero probability is allowed. (A) sums to 1.1, (B) has a negative entry, (D) sums to 0.6.

A generated answer earns reward 1 with probability 0.3 and 0 otherwise. What is the expected reward?

  1. $0.3$
  2. $1$
  3. $0.5$
  4. $0$, because you can't earn 0.3
$1 \times 0.3 + 0 \times 0.7 = 0.3$. Like the die's 3.5, an expectation needn't be a possible outcome. It's the long-run average reward per answer.

It's cloudy on 40% of days, it rains on 24% of days, and it only ever rains when cloudy. What is $P(\text{rain} \mid \text{cloudy})$?

  1. $0.24$
  2. $0.096$
  3. $0.16$
  4. $0.6$
$P(\text{rain and cloudy}) / P(\text{cloudy}) = 0.24 / 0.40 = 0.6$. (0.096 multiplies instead of dividing.)

(From P1.3) Training increases $\ln p$ for the correct token. What is the derivative $\frac{d}{dp}\ln p$ at $p = 0.1$, and what does it suggest?

  1. $0.1$: low-probability tokens barely matter
  2. $10$: a correct token the model thought unlikely gets a strong push
  3. $-2.3$: the gradient is always negative
  4. $1$: every token gets the same push
$\frac{d}{dp}\ln p = \frac{1}{p} = 10$ at $p = 0.1$, versus about 1.1 at $p = 0.9$. The log objective pushes hardest exactly where the model was most wrong.

Key takeaways

  • A discrete distribution: non-negative probabilities that sum to 1.
  • Expectation $\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x)$ is a probability-weighted average, the long-run mean.
  • Sampling: a uniform random number picks a slice of $[0, 1)$; likelier outcomes have wider slices.
  • An LLM outputs $P(\text{next token} \mid \text{context})$; chaining these gives a sequence's probability.
  • Products of probabilities underflow, so we add log-probabilities. The negative average is the training loss.
