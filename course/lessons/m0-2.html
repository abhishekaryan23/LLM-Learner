The learning loop

Every lesson in this course runs the same loop: guess, learn, explain, quiz, lock in, review. Once you know the loop, you can move through any lesson, from gradient descent to DPO, on autopilot and spend your energy on the ideas.

By the end you can

  • Name the six stages of a lesson and what each one is for.
  • Use animations, ELI5 boxes and Colab notebooks the way they were designed to be used.
  • Plan a study session that fits in 20–40 minutes.
Warm-up · guess before you learn

An animation explains a 6-step process. What's the best way to watch it?

  1. Let it autoplay start to finish, twice
  2. Step through it yourself, predicting each next step before you tap
  3. Skip it and read the text instead
  4. Watch it at double speed to save time
Controlling the pace (Mayer's segmenting principle) and predicting before you see each step (a mini retrieval attempt) both help. Autoplay is there for a quick recap.

You open the course after two days away. What should you do first?

  1. Clear your due review cards, then start the next lesson
  2. Re-read the last lesson from the top
  3. Start the next lesson; reviews can wait until the weekend
  4. Skip ahead to the topic you find most interesting
Reviews are scheduled for the moment they help most: just as you're about to forget. Doing them first also warms up the ideas the next lesson builds on.

How to use each part

Animations

Animations are interactive diagrams, not videos. Use ◀ ▶ (or swipe on a phone or iPad) to move one idea at a time, the slider to scrub, and the dots to jump. Pause and predict before you tap "next": the small effort of guessing is what turns watching into learning. If your device has "reduce motion" switched on, steps change instantly instead of animating.

Explain like I'm 5

Hard topics have a dashed Explain like I'm 5 box with an everyday analogy and an illustration. Open it after trying the real explanation. Analogies are scaffolding: they help you hang the precise idea somewhere, but every analogy breaks eventually. The lesson text tells you where.

Explain like I'm 5: why a loop?

Learning to ride a bike isn't "watch someone ride, once." You try, wobble, get a tip, try again tomorrow, and a week later you just can. Every lesson here is one lap of that: try → see how → say it yourself → test → come back later.

Worked, then faded examples

Math-heavy lessons show one example fully solved, then a near-identical one with a few steps left blank for you. This fading moves you from studying a solution to producing one, and it's gentler than a blank page.

Colab notebooks (optional)

Some lessons have an Open in Colab button. It opens a Python notebook in Google Colab, free in your browser with no install. Each notebook has small exercises with # TODO markers, check cells that tell you if you got it right, and full solutions at the bottom. They're optional. The course works without them, but writing ten lines of code is the fastest way to discover what you didn't understand.

A good session (20–40 min): ① clear due reviews (5–10 min) → ② one new lesson (10–15 min) → ③ optionally its notebook. One or two lessons a day beats a weekend marathon: sleep is when the brain consolidates new memories.

Prerequisites: take only what you need

The Prerequisites track (math, ML basics, neural nets, PyTorch) is there so nobody gets stuck later. If a topic is already solid for you, skim its key takeaways and do its quiz. If you ace the quiz, move on. If not, that lesson is exactly where your time pays off.

Check yourself

What is the main purpose of the "Explain it back" box?

  1. To grade you
  2. To save notes for later
  3. To make you put the idea in your own words and connect it to what you know
  4. To slow you down so the lesson lasts longer
That's elaboration plus retrieval: generating an explanation exposes gaps that re-reading hides.

When should you open an ELI5 box?

  1. Before reading anything else in the section
  2. After attempting the real explanation, as a second angle
  3. Only if you're a beginner
  4. Never; analogies are inaccurate
Analogies give you a hook, but the precise idea is what you need in practice. Try the real version first, then use the analogy to make it click.
