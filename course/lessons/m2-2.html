The next-token objective

Lesson 2 of Pretraining

Pretraining has one job, repeated trillions of times: given the text so far, predict the next token. No labels, no human annotators. This lesson shows how a single sentence turns into many training examples, and how the loss you see in every training curve is computed.

By the end you can

  • Build inputs and shift-by-one targets from raw text, and explain teacher forcing.
  • Compute the average cross-entropy loss of a short sequence by hand.
  • Turn a loss into a perplexity, and sanity-check the loss at initialization.

Warm-up · guess before you learn

Guess first. Being wrong now is part of how it sticks.

Where do the "correct answers" (labels) come from when an LLM is pretrained?

  1. Human annotators label each sentence
  2. A smaller model labels the data first
  3. The text itself: the label at each position is simply the next token
  4. There are no correct answers; pretraining is unsupervised clustering
This is self-supervised learning: the targets are cut from the same text as the inputs. That's why any text on the internet can be training data.

From one 6-token sentence, how many next-token predictions can a model be trained on in a single forward pass?

  1. 1, the last token only
  2. 5, one for every position that has a next token
  3. 6
  4. 36, every pair of tokens
Every position with a following token is a training example, and the causal mask lets all of them be computed at once.

The text is its own label

A language model assigns a probability to the next token given all the previous ones: $p_\theta(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_t)$. To train it, we don't need anyone to write answers. Take any text, hide what comes next, and check the model's guess against what actually came next. That's self-supervised learning: supervised learning where the labels are carved out of the input.

Shift by one, train every position at once

In code, the targets are just the inputs shifted one place to the left. For a batch of token ids tokens with shape (batch, T + 1):

inputs  = tokens[:, :-1]          # everything but the last token
targets = tokens[:, 1:]           # everything but the first
logits  = model(inputs)           # (batch, T, vocab): one prediction per position
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.reshape(-1, vocab), targets.reshape(-1))

Two ideas make this efficient:

  • The causal mask (M1.1). Position $t$ can only attend to positions $\le t$, so predicting token $t+1$ never peeks at it. That makes it legal to compute all $T$ predictions in one forward pass instead of $T$ separate ones.
  • Teacher forcing. The inputs are always the real tokens, never the model's own guesses. Even if the model would have predicted "dog" after "the", position 2 still reads "cat". This keeps training parallel and stable. The flip side is a small mismatch with generation, where the model does read its own outputs (sometimes called exposure bias).

Real pretraining doesn't use one sentence at a time. Documents are concatenated, separated by an end-of-text token, and cut into fixed-length windows (typically a few thousand tokens), so every window is full and every position contributes to the loss.

The loss: average cross-entropy

For a window of tokens $x_1, \dots, x_{T+1}$, the pretraining loss is

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \ln p_\theta\!\left(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_t\right)$$

In words: at each of the $T$ positions, take the probability $p_\theta$ that the model (with parameters $\theta$) gave to the token that really came next, take its natural log, flip the sign, and average. This is exactly the cross-entropy from P1.6 between the one-hot "true next token" distribution and the model's softmax output: with a one-hot target, $-\sum_k y_k \ln p_k$ keeps only the term for the correct token. In practice it is also averaged over every window in the batch.

  • Confident and right ($p = 0.9$): loss $-\ln 0.9 \approx 0.11$.
  • Unsure ($p = 0.1$): loss $\approx 2.30$.
  • Confident and wrong ($p = 0.001$): loss $\approx 6.91$. The log punishes confident mistakes hard.

With natural logs the loss is in nats. Divide by $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ to get bits.

Explain like I'm 5

Two friends play a game with a story told on picture cards. One friend flips the cards over one at a time and the other has to guess what the next card shows before it's turned.

A good guess glows: "I was pretty sure it would be the cat!" A bad guess is a big surprise. After thousands of stories, the guesser gets really good, because to guess well you have to understand the story.

Where it breaks: the model doesn't make one guess. It spreads its bets over every possible card, and it's scored on how much it bet on the right one.

Worked example

A three-position window. The model gives the correct next tokens probabilities $0.2$, $0.5$ and $0.8$.

  1. Per-position losses: $-\ln 0.2 \approx 1.609$, $-\ln 0.5 \approx 0.693$, $-\ln 0.8 \approx 0.223$.
  2. Sum: $1.609 + 0.693 + 0.223 = 2.525$.
  3. Average: $2.525 / 3 \approx 0.842$ nats. That's the loss.
  4. Perplexity: $e^{0.842} \approx 2.32$.

Perplexity, and a sanity check at step 0

Perplexity is $e^{\mathcal{L}}$. It reads as an "effective number of choices": a perplexity of 4.31 means the model is, on average, as unsure as if it were picking uniformly among about 4 tokens. Lesson M4.1 Perplexity covers its uses and pitfalls (it depends on the tokenizer, for one).

A freshly initialized model spreads probability roughly evenly over its vocabulary of $V$ tokens, so every position costs about $-\ln(1/V) = \ln V$. For Llama 3's vocabulary of 128,256 tokens, that's $\ln 128{,}256 \approx 11.76$ nats. If your step-0 loss is far from $\ln V$, something is broken (initialization, masking or the target shift) before training has even begun.

Why this matters in practice: the falling curve in every pretraining report is this number, the average next-token cross-entropy, usually measured on held-out text. Scaling laws (M2.4) predict it, optimizers (M2.3) minimize it, and post-training (M3) reuses the same loss for supervised fine-tuning, only computed on the response tokens instead of everything.
Explain it back · in two or three sentences

How does one window of raw text become a loss value during pretraining?

Model answer: The window is split into inputs (all tokens but the last) and targets (the same tokens shifted one to the left). With a causal mask and teacher forcing, the model predicts a distribution at every position in one pass; each position's loss is −ln of the probability given to the true next token, and the loss is the average over all positions.
Check yourself

A training window holds 4,097 tokens. How many next-token predictions does it contribute to the loss?

  1. 1
  2. 4,096
  3. 4,097
  4. 4,096², one per pair of positions
Every token except the last has a next token to predict: 4,097 − 1 = 4,096. That's why windows are often stored with one extra token.

What does teacher forcing mean?

  1. A larger "teacher" model labels the training data
  2. The learning rate is forced to stay constant
  3. The model is trained on its own generated text
  4. During training, each position reads the true previous tokens, not the model's own earlier predictions
The real text is "forced" in as input at every position. That makes all positions independent given the data, so they can be trained in parallel.

Linking back to M1.1: someone forgets the causal mask during pretraining. What happens?

  1. The training loss drops to nearly zero because each position can see its own target, and the model is useless for generation
  2. Training is slower but the model ends up the same
  3. The loss gets stuck at $\ln V$
  4. Nothing; the mask only matters at inference
Without the mask, position $t$ can attend to token $t+1$, which is its target. The model learns to copy, the loss looks wonderful, and at generation time (when there is no next token to copy) it falls apart.

A model has a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens (GPT-2's). Roughly what loss should you see at step 0 with a sensible random initialization?

  1. About 0
  2. About 1
  3. About 10.8 nats
  4. About 50,257
Near-uniform predictions give $-\ln(1/V) = \ln 50{,}257 \approx 10.8$ nats per position, a perplexity of about $V$ itself.

Key takeaways

  • Pretraining is self-supervised: the target at each position is the next token of the same text.
  • Targets are the inputs shifted by one; the causal mask and teacher forcing let every position train in parallel.
  • Loss $= -\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \ln p_\theta(x_{t+1}\mid x_{\le t})$, the average cross-entropy over all positions.
  • Perplexity $= e^{\text{loss}}$; at initialization the loss should be about $\ln V$.
